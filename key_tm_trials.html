Key Trials in Transfusion Medicine

Reference page summarizing major trials that commonly inform transfusion medicine recommendations in cardiac surgery, non-cardiac surgery, critical care, acute coronary syndrome, obstetrics, hematologic malignancy, gastrointestinal bleeding, intracranial hemorrhage, and trauma.

This page is designed as a practical bedside-style review with emphasis on clinically useful take-home messages rather than exhaustive trial appraisal.

Restrictive RBC transfusion

Across cardiac surgery, non-cardiac surgery, ICU care, septic shock, GI bleeding, and many hematology settings, restrictive strategies are generally safe and often reduce blood use without worsening major outcomes.

TXA is setting-specific

TXA has clear benefit in some bleeding settings such as trauma and established postpartum hemorrhage, but not in GI bleeding, and prophylactic benefit varies by surgical context.

Platelets are not always helpful

Prophylactic platelet transfusion remains standard in many hypoproliferative thrombocytopenia settings, but platelet transfusion for antiplatelet-associated spontaneous ICH is not supported.

Factor concentrates are increasingly relevant

Selected trials support fibrinogen concentrate or coagulation factor concentrate strategies as reasonable or superior alternatives to traditional plasma-based approaches in specific bleeding scenarios.

Cardiac surgery

4 key trials

PCC vs Frozen Plasma

Smith et al., JAMA Surgery, 2022

Population: Cardiac surgery patients with excessive microvascular bleeding and prolonged PT/INR.

Intervention vs comparison: PCC 15 IU/kg vs frozen plasma 10–15 mL/kg.

  • Similar postoperative chest tube output.
  • Better PT/INR correction with PCC.
  • No significant difference in transfusion rates or major safety signals.
Take-home: PCC appears comparable to frozen plasma for postoperative cardiac surgery coagulopathy, with faster INR correction but without clear superiority in bleeding outcomes.

FIBRES

Callum et al., JAMA, 2019

Population: Bleeding patients within 24 hours after CABG with hypofibrinogenemia.

Intervention vs comparison: Fibrinogen concentrate 4 g vs cryoprecipitate 10 units.

  • Non-inferior for total blood components transfused in 24 hours.
  • No significant difference in adverse events, death, or thrombosis.
Take-home: Fibrinogen concentrate is non-inferior to cryoprecipitate for bleeding and hypofibrinogenemia after CABG.

TRICS-III

Mazer et al., NEJM, 2017

Population: Higher-risk patients undergoing cardiac surgery.

Strategy: Restrictive target Hb 75 g/L vs liberal target Hb 95 g/L in OR/ICU and 85 g/L on ward.

  • Restrictive strategy was non-inferior for death, MI, or dialysis.
  • Fewer patients received RBC transfusion in the restrictive arm.
Take-home: A 75 g/L threshold is reasonable in stable post-cardiac surgery patients, especially when not actively ischemic or unstable.

ATACAS

Myles et al., NEJM, 2017

Population: High-risk patients undergoing CABG.

Intervention vs comparison: Prophylactic TXA vs placebo.

  • Reduced transfusion exposure and return to the OR.
  • No reduction in death or thrombotic events.
  • Higher seizure risk, particularly relevant with high-dose regimens.
Take-home: TXA prophylaxis can reduce bleeding/transfusion in cardiac surgery, but seizure risk must be weighed.

Non-cardiac surgery

2 key trials

POISE-3

Devereaux et al., NEJM, 2022

Population: Patients undergoing non-cardiac surgery at risk of bleeding and cardiovascular complications.

Intervention vs comparison: TXA 1 g at start and end of surgery vs placebo.

  • Reduced composite bleeding outcome.
  • Did not demonstrate non-inferiority for the composite cardiovascular outcome.
Take-home: Prophylactic TXA reduces bleeding in major non-cardiac surgery, but clinicians should still consider the unresolved cardiovascular non-inferiority question.

FOCUS

Carson et al., NEJM, 2011

Population: Older patients with cardiovascular risk after hip fracture surgery.

Strategy: Liberal transfusion target >100 g/L vs restrictive target >80 g/L.

  • No improvement in death or walking ability at 60 days with liberal transfusion.
  • Substantially more RBC use in the liberal arm.
Take-home: Liberal transfusion after orthopedic surgery does not improve recovery and increases blood utilization.

Critically ill patients

2 key trials

TRICC

Hebert et al., NEJM, 1999

Population: Euvolemic critically ill ICU patients with anemia and no active bleeding.

Strategy: Hb target 70 g/L vs 100 g/L.

  • Restrictive strategy was at least as effective overall.
  • Lower hospital mortality and fewer cardiac events were seen in some analyses.
  • Marked reduction in RBC use.
Take-home: Restrictive transfusion is standard for many stable ICU patients, with caution in acute coronary ischemia.

TRISS

Holst et al., NEJM, 2014

Population: Septic shock patients with Hb ≤90 g/L.

Strategy: Threshold 70 g/L vs 90 g/L.

  • No significant difference in 90-day mortality or ischemic events.
  • Restrictive strategy used substantially fewer transfusions.
Take-home: In septic shock, a 70 g/L threshold is generally appropriate unless other clinical factors justify otherwise.

Acute coronary syndrome

1 key trial

REALITY

Ducrocq et al., JAMA, 2021

Population: Acute myocardial infarction with hemoglobin 70–100 g/L.

Strategy: Liberal transfusion target >100 g/L vs restrictive target >80 g/L.

  • Restrictive strategy was non-inferior for 30-day major adverse cardiovascular events.
  • Far fewer patients received transfusion in the restrictive arm.
Take-home: In ACS, available evidence supports at least an 80 g/L threshold as a reasonable restrictive strategy, though this remains a nuanced area.

Obstetrics and gynecology

4 key trials

TRAAP

Sentilhes et al., NEJM, 2018

Population: Vaginal delivery.

Intervention vs comparison: Prophylactic TXA vs placebo immediately postpartum.

  • No significant reduction in the primary PPH endpoint.
  • Some secondary bleeding-related measures favored TXA.
  • No important thrombotic signal.
Take-home: Prophylactic TXA after vaginal delivery is not clearly established by this trial.

TRAAP2

Sentilhes et al., NEJM, 2021

Population: Cesarean delivery.

Intervention vs comparison: Prophylactic TXA vs placebo after birth.

  • Reduced measured postpartum hemorrhage endpoint.
  • No clear improvement in clinically important transfusion outcomes.
  • More nausea and vomiting with TXA.
Take-home: TXA after C-section reduces measured blood loss outcomes, but benefit on major clinical endpoints is less certain.

WOMAN

Shakur et al., Lancet, 2017

Population: Established postpartum hemorrhage after vaginal birth or cesarean delivery.

Intervention vs comparison: TXA 1 g IV vs placebo.

  • Reduced death due to bleeding, especially when given within 3 hours of birth.
  • No clear reduction in hysterectomy or transfusion requirement.
Take-home: Give TXA early in established postpartum hemorrhage, ideally within 3 hours.

Heavy menstrual bleeding

Lukes et al., Obstetrics & Gynecology, 2010

Population: Women with heavy menstrual bleeding.

Intervention vs comparison: Oral TXA vs placebo.

  • Significant reduction in menstrual blood loss.
  • Improved health-related quality of life.
Take-home: TXA is an effective non-hormonal option for heavy menstrual bleeding.

Hematologic malignancy

3 key trials

TRIST

Tay et al., Journal of Clinical Oncology, 2020

Population: Hematopoietic cell transplant recipients.

Strategy: Hb target 70 g/L vs 90 g/L.

  • Restrictive transfusion was non-inferior for quality of life.
  • No major clinical outcome difference.
  • Fewer RBC transfusions with the restrictive approach.
Take-home: In many transplant patients, a 70 g/L RBC threshold is reasonable.

TOPPS

Stanworth et al., NEJM, 2013

Population: Hematology patients with thrombocytopenia from chemotherapy or stem cell transplant.

Strategy: No prophylactic platelet transfusion vs prophylactic transfusion at platelet count <10 × 109/L.

  • Non-inferiority was not shown for withholding prophylactic platelets.
  • More bleeding days and earlier bleeding without prophylaxis.
Take-home: Continue prophylactic platelet transfusion in most hypoproliferative thrombocytopenia settings.

a-TREAT

Gernsheimer et al., Blood, 2020

Population: Hematologic malignancy patients with hypoproliferative thrombocytopenia.

Intervention vs comparison: Prophylactic TXA vs placebo.

  • No reduction in WHO grade 2+ bleeding.
  • No decrease in platelet transfusion requirement.
Take-home: Routine prophylactic TXA is not supported in this setting.

Gastrointestinal bleeding

2 key trials

Villanueva Trial

Villanueva et al., NEJM, 2013

Population: Acute upper GI bleeding.

Strategy: Restrictive transfusion target 70 g/L vs liberal target 90 g/L.

  • Improved 6-week survival with the restrictive strategy.
  • Reduced further bleeding.
  • Benefit appeared strongest in Child-Pugh A/B cirrhosis.
Take-home: In stable upper GI bleeding, targeting 70 g/L is preferred over routine liberal transfusion.

HALT-IT

Roberts et al., Lancet, 2020

Population: GI bleeding.

Intervention vs comparison: High-dose TXA vs placebo.

  • No reduction in death from bleeding.
  • Higher rate of thromboembolic events with TXA.
Take-home: TXA should not be routinely added for GI bleeding.

Intracranial hemorrhage

2 key trials

PATCH

Baharoglu et al., Lancet, 2016

Population: Spontaneous supratentorial ICH in patients recently taking antiplatelet agents.

Intervention vs comparison: Platelet transfusion vs standard care.

  • Higher risk of death or dependence at 3 months in the platelet transfusion arm.
Take-home: Do not routinely give platelets for spontaneous antiplatelet-associated ICH.

TICH-2

Sprigg et al., Lancet, 2018

Population: ICH within 8 hours of onset.

Intervention vs comparison: TXA vs placebo.

  • No significant improvement in 90-day functional outcome.
  • No clear mortality or length-of-stay benefit.
  • Possible signal when treated earlier, with reassuring safety.
Take-home: TXA has not shown major functional benefit in ICH, but early use may still be considered in selected cases.

Trauma

4 key trials

Robertson Head Injury Trial

Robertson et al., JAMA, 2014

Population: Severe traumatic brain injury.

Strategy: Hb target 70 g/L vs 100 g/L.

  • No functional recovery advantage with the liberal strategy.
  • More thromboembolic events with target 100 g/L.
Take-home: Liberal transfusion to 100 g/L after traumatic brain injury does not improve recovery and may worsen safety.

CRASH-2

Lancet, 2010

Population: Trauma patients at risk of significant hemorrhage.

Intervention vs comparison: TXA vs placebo.

  • Reduced in-hospital death, particularly in bleeding patients.
  • No major reduction in blood product use.
Take-home: TXA remains part of standard management for bleeding trauma, especially when given early.

CRASH-3

Lancet, 2019

Population: Traumatic brain injury treated within 3 hours of injury.

Intervention vs comparison: TXA vs placebo.

  • Overall mortality signal was modest.
  • Benefit appeared greater in mild-to-moderate TBI.
Take-home: Early TXA is reasonable in selected TBI patients given favorable safety and possible survival benefit.

RETIC

Innerhofer et al., Lancet Haematology, 2017

Population: Bleeding trauma patients with ROTEM-defined coagulopathy.

Intervention vs comparison: Coagulation factor concentrate strategy (often fibrinogen concentrate-based) vs frozen plasma.

  • Less rescue therapy.
  • Lower need for massive transfusion.
  • Trial stopped early because of superiority signal.
Take-home: In trauma-associated coagulopathy, factor concentrate-based strategies may outperform plasma in selected settings.

Quick practice points

High-yield summary

Cardiac surgery

Restrictive RBC threshold around 75 g/L is generally acceptable in stable patients. Fibrinogen concentrate is a reasonable alternative to cryoprecipitate. TXA lowers bleeding/transfusion but may increase seizures.

ICU / septic shock

Use restrictive RBC transfusion, often around 70 g/L, unless there is active ischemia or another compelling indication.

ACS

Evidence supports a more cautious restrictive approach, often around 80 g/L rather than very liberal transfusion.

GI bleeding

Restrictive transfusion is preferred. Do not routinely add TXA.

PPH

Use TXA early once postpartum hemorrhage is present. Prophylactic use remains more nuanced.

Heme malignancy

Restrictive RBC transfusion is acceptable; continue prophylactic platelets in most hypoproliferative thrombocytopenia settings; do not use routine prophylactic TXA.

ICH

Platelets are not recommended for spontaneous antiplatelet-associated ICH. TXA has limited proven benefit.

Trauma

Give TXA early in bleeding trauma. Liberal RBC transfusion to 100 g/L in TBI is not beneficial and may increase harm.

Severe Neonatal Thrombocytopenia in the Setting of Maternal Immune Thrombocytopenia

Diagnostic distinction between autoimmune thrombocytopenia, FNAIT, and apparent rapid IVIG response

Clinical presentation

A 34-year-old woman underwent uncomplicated elective Cesarean delivery at term. Postpartum testing showed platelets 24 × 10⁹/L, compared with 106 × 10⁹/L approximately one month earlier. Hemoglobin was 104 g/L, MCV 60 fL, WBC 18.2 × 10⁹/L and neutrophils 15.6 × 10⁹/L. INR 0.88, aPTT 31.2 seconds and fibrinogen 4.1 g/L were unremarkable. There was no bleeding, hypertension, or clinical evidence of preeclampsia or HELLP syndrome. Recent upper respiratory infection and amoxicillin exposure were reported. Beta-thalassemia trait was known; concomitant iron deficiency was possible.

Maternal peripheral blood film

Marked thrombocytopenia with giant platelets, without increased schistocytes. Marked microcytosis was present. The findings supported a peripheral platelet destruction/turnover process, although giant platelets are not specific for immune thrombocytopenia (ITP). Maternal ITP was clinically favored; drug-associated thrombocytopenia remained a consideration.

Neonatal course

The term infant was clinically well without petechiae, bruising, bleeding, or neurologic abnormalities. Severe thrombocytopenia was reported, including an initial value of 24 × 10⁹/L and a subsequent venous value of 8 × 10⁹/L. The latter was confirmed on direct blood film review: markedly reduced platelets without clumping. The infant was transferred to the NICU and received IVIG 1 g/kg. Platelets were prepared but not transfused after count normalization.

Diagnostic interpretation

Severe neonatal immune-mediated thrombocytopenia is favored in the setting of maternal suspected ITP. Transplacental maternal platelet autoantibodies provide a plausible mechanism. However, the increase from 8 to 198 × 10⁹/L within 8 h 34 min (and less than four hours after the recorded IVIG administration) is exceptionally rapid for a typical IVIG response. Temporal association alone cannot prove treatment causality.

Direct smear confirmation without clumping argues against platelet-clumping pseudothrombocytopenia, although other preanalytical or specimen discrepancies cannot be fully excluded. Fetal/neonatal alloimmune thrombocytopenia (FNAIT) remains an important differential diagnosis, especially given the profound nadir. Maternal ITP and FNAIT are not mutually exclusive; response to IVIG does not distinguish them.

Management and follow-up considerations

  • Serial neonatal platelet counts, including surveillance for a delayed nadir or recurrence after IVIG.
  • Cranial ultrasonography to assess for occult intracranial hemorrhage given the reported nadir.
  • Consider maternal platelet antibody testing and parental/neonatal HPA genotyping through a reference laboratory if FNAIT is suspected.
  • When urgent platelet transfusion is required for suspected FNAIT, HPA-compatible platelets are preferred if promptly available; do not delay life-saving transfusion while awaiting matched units.
  • Neonatal hematology follow-up until platelet counts remain stable without treatment.
Teaching point. Morphologic confirmation makes clumping artifact unlikely, but does not establish the immune mechanism. Maternal ITP supports neonatal autoimmune thrombocytopenia; profound neonatal thrombocytopenia still warrants consideration of FNAIT. A rapid post-IVIG count rise should not be interpreted as a diagnostic test.

Massive Hemorrhage Protocol (MHP)

Practical reference page for definition, pathophysiology, transfusion targets, initial product strategy, adjunctive therapy, laboratory monitoring, and selected evidence relevant to massive hemorrhage and emergency transfusion.

Definition of Massive Transfusion / Massive Hemorrhage

Retrospective definitions

  • Administration of 10 units of RBC or replacement of more than one blood volume within 24 hours.

Dynamic definitions

  • Transfusion of 3 units of RBC within 1 hour with ongoing need anticipated.
  • UHN MHP trigger: 4 units within 1 hour.

Pediatric definitions

  • Transfusion support to replace ongoing blood loss of >10% total blood volume per minute.
  • Transfusion of >100% total blood volume in 24 hours.
  • Transfusion of >50% total blood volume in 3 hours.
In practice, MHP is usually activated on the basis of clinical trajectory rather than waiting for retrospective thresholds to be met.

Pathophysiology of Coagulopathy in Massive Hemorrhage

Major bleeding is sustained not only by blood loss itself, but also by rapidly evolving coagulopathy.

  1. Loss of clotting factors through ongoing hemorrhage.
  2. Activation of coagulation from traumatic exposure of subendothelium.
  3. Dilutional coagulopathy from crystalloids, colloids, and RBC-heavy resuscitation.
  4. Some resuscitation fluids may have anticoagulant and anti-platelet effects.
  5. Exposure of tissue plasminogen activator leading to hyperfibrinolysis.
  6. Hypothermia.
  7. Acidosis.
  8. Acute traumatic coagulopathy.

Acute traumatic coagulopathy

Acute traumatic coagulopathy is an impairment in hemostasis seen early after major injury that is not fully explained by dilution, hypothermia, or acidosis alone. Its etiology remains incompletely understood, but proposed mechanisms include protein C pathway activation, endogenous anticoagulation, and enhanced fibrinolysis.

Effects of hypothermia

  • Reduces enzymatic activity of coagulation proteins, especially serine proteases.
  • Impairs von Willebrand factor–platelet interactions.
  • Net result: worsened bleeding.

Effects of acidosis

  • Impairs formation of the tenase and prothrombinase complexes on platelet surfaces.
  • Decreases fibrin polymerization.
  • Net result: worsened bleeding.
Key principle: transfusion alone will not correct MHP unless hemorrhage control, warming, calcium replacement, and correction of severe acidosis are pursued simultaneously.

Initial Transfusion Management

In the rapidly bleeding patient, when laboratory-guided resuscitation is not yet possible, initial management should begin with immediate RBC support followed by empiric component therapy.

1. Activate MHP early
Communicate anticipated severity, ongoing bleeding, and need for repeated product release.
2. Start emergency RBC transfusion
Use uncrossmatched product when required, then transition to group-specific components as soon as current typing is available.
3. Empiric balanced resuscitation
A practical early approach is RBC:plasma = 2:1 until laboratory or viscoelastic guidance becomes available.
4. Add platelets and fibrinogen support promptly
Avoid isolated RBC-heavy resuscitation, which worsens dilutional coagulopathy.
5. Correct contributors to coagulopathy
Warm the patient, monitor ionized calcium, limit severe acidemia, and control the source of bleeding.
ABO issue: historical ABO/Rh type alone should not be used for current group-specific emergency release. If a current type is not yet available, issue emergency-compatible product and switch to group-specific product once current typing is confirmed.

Laboratory-Guided Resuscitation Targets

Minimal target thresholds during MHP include the following:

Hemoglobin Maintain >80 g/L.
INR Maintain <1.8, using plasma or PCC where appropriate.
Fibrinogen Maintain >1.5 g/L, using cryoprecipitate or fibrinogen concentrate.
Platelets Maintain >50 × 109/L.
UHN practice: target >100 × 109/L in CNS injury or on ECLS.
Ionized calcium Maintain >1.15 mmol/L.
Where available, viscoelastic testing may be used to guide therapy, but these standard component targets remain practical bedside anchors.

Fibrinogen replacement

  • Cryoprecipitate remains a common source of fibrinogen replacement.
  • Fibrinogen concentrate 4 g is a reasonable alternative and is approximately equivalent to 10 units of cryoprecipitate.

PCC substitution when plasma is not readily available

At institutions lacking sufficient resources to issue plasma rapidly, PCC 2000 IU may be used for factor replacement. Unless fibrinogen is already known to be >1.5 g/L, this should be accompanied by fibrinogen replacement, preferably fibrinogen concentrate.

Laboratory Monitoring in MHP

The recommended minimum laboratory testing at each draw includes:

  • CBC
  • INR
  • aPTT (baseline only in some protocols)
  • Fibrinogen
  • Electrolytes
  • Ionized calcium
  • Blood gas, including pH and base excess
  • Lactate

Suggested frequency

  • Core hemostatic testing approximately every 60 minutes during active MHP.

UHN minimum monitoring framework

Test group Suggested interval
CBC, INR, fibrinogen, ABG, aPTT (baseline only) Every 60 minutes at minimum
Electrolytes, creatinine, ALT, ionized calcium, magnesium, lactate Every 4 hours or after every 20 blood components administered, at minimum
Traditional coagulation assays may lag behind the clinical state. Laboratory testing should support, not delay, hemorrhage control and empiric resuscitation.

Tranexamic Acid (TXA)

TXA should be administered as soon as intravenous or intraosseous access is achieved, ideally within 3 hours of injury or within 3 hours of MHP activation in other hemorrhaging patients.

Adult dosing example

  • Standard simplified adult protocol: 1 g over 10 minutes, followed by 1 g over 8 hours.
  • UHN weight-based protocol: 10 mg/kg bolus over 15 minutes, followed by 1 mg/kg/h infusion for up to 10 hours.

Selected Evidence: Balanced Ratios and Restrictive RBC Practice

PROPPR trial

The PROPPR trial compared 1:1:1 versus 1:1:2 ratios of plasma:platelets:RBCs in severe trauma resuscitation.

Arm Component packs
1:1:1 6 units plasma + 1 dose platelets + 6 units RBC
1:1:2 Alternating packs containing 3 units plasma + 0 dose platelets + 6 units RBC, then 3 units plasma + 1 dose platelets + 6 units RBC
  • At 3 hours, 1:1:1 reduced hemorrhagic death compared with 1:1:2.
  • No clear difference in overall mortality at 24 hours or 30 days.
Canadian practical equivalence: a commonly used bedside approximation of 1:1:1 is often described as 4 RBC : 4 plasma : 1 adult platelet dose. Exact local packaging and release patterns vary by institution.

General RBC transfusion evidence outside active MHP

Jeannie Callum teaching points strongly support a restrictive transfusion approach for most non-massively bleeding settings. These principles are important, but they should not be misapplied to an actively exsanguinating patient under MHP.

  • Restrictive triggers are generally favored over liberal ones in stable patients.
  • Typical thresholds are around 70 g/L for many stable inpatients and 80 g/L in selected cardiac or orthopedic settings.
  • In active hemorrhage, transfusion should be driven by clinical bleeding severity, hemodynamics, and MHP targets rather than single-unit restrictive logic.

Important Metabolic Issues in Massive Transfusion

Citrate load

Blood components contain citrate anticoagulant. During massive transfusion, citrate delivery may exceed the patient’s metabolic clearance, especially in shock or liver dysfunction.

Complications of citrate infusion

  • Hypocalcemia due to citrate binding of ionized calcium.
  • Metabolic alkalosis due to citrate metabolism.

Why hypocalcemia matters

  • Reduces effective coagulation.
  • May cause paresthesias, hypotension, or cardiac arrhythmias.
  • Supports the need for serial monitoring and calcium replacement during MHP.

General RBC Practice Pearls

  • Outside active bleeding, transfuse one unit at a time and reassess.
  • Consider iron, B12, folate, or erythropoietin when appropriate.
  • In females of childbearing potential, consider antigen-selection strategies that reduce alloimmunization risk.
  • At high TACO risk, consider slower infusion and closer volume assessment.
These principles apply mainly to non-massive, non-exsanguinating settings and should not delay MHP treatment in active hemorrhage.

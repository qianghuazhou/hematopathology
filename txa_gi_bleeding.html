Tranexamic Acid for Gastrointestinal Bleeding

Practical reference page summarizing the role of tranexamic acid (TXA) in lower GI bleeding, key trial data, anticoagulant-associated bleeding considerations, the narrow hereditary hemorrhagic telangiectasia exception, and a guideline-oriented management framework.

Clinical Bottom Line

TXA should not be used for acute lower gastrointestinal bleeding (LGIB) outside of a clinical trial. The source document states that the 2023 ACG guideline recommends against antifibrinolytic agents such as TXA in LGIB, and that HALT-IT plus LGIB-specific trials showed no improvement in mortality, transfusion requirements, rebleeding, or intervention rates, with increased venous thromboembolism and seizures.

The recommendation applies broadly, including patients with severe bleeding and those receiving anticoagulants. The uploaded review repeatedly emphasizes that TXA does not improve clinically meaningful outcomes in acute LGIB and adds harm risk.

Key Evidence

HALT-IT

HALT-IT is presented as the largest and most definitive randomized trial of TXA in GI bleeding, enrolling 12,009 adults with significant upper or lower GI bleeding. Patients received 1 g IV loading followed by 3 g over 24 hours, or placebo.

Outcome TXA vs placebo Interpretation
Death due to bleeding within 5 days 3.7% vs 3.8%; RR 0.99 (95% CI 0.82–1.18) No mortality benefit
Venous thromboembolism 0.8% vs 0.4%; RR 1.85 (95% CI 1.15–2.98) Increased risk
Seizure 0.6% vs 0.4%; RR 1.73 (95% CI 1.03–2.93) Increased risk
Rebleeding / intervention / transfusion No significant reduction No meaningful efficacy signal

LGIB-specific randomized trials

The uploaded summary also cites two lower-GI-specific randomized trials that showed no significant benefit in hemoglobin drop, transfusion needs, intervention rates, or hospital stay.

Why TXA Is Not Recommended in Acute LGIB

  • No demonstrated reduction in death due to bleeding.
  • No convincing benefit for transfusion requirement, rebleeding, or procedural need.
  • Clear signal for harm, especially venous thromboembolism and seizure.
  • Guideline consensus in the uploaded document recommends avoiding antifibrinolytics such as TXA in acute LGIB.

Do not use TXA as salvage therapy for severe or refractory acute LGIB according to the uploaded review. The document explicitly states that even patients at risk of bleeding to death in HALT-IT did not derive benefit.

Anticoagulant-Associated Lower GI Bleeding

The uploaded review specifically addresses anticoagulated patients and concludes that TXA does not improve outcomes and may be particularly problematic because it adds prothrombotic risk without correcting the underlying anticoagulant-related hemostatic defect.

Recommended approach

  • Hold the anticoagulant for significant bleeding requiring hospitalization.
  • Provide hemodynamic resuscitation with IV fluids and blood products as needed.
  • Use selective reversal only for life-threatening bleeding or persistent hemodynamic instability.
  • Proceed to endoscopic or radiologic management once clinically appropriate.
  • Avoid TXA.

Exception: Hereditary Hemorrhagic Telangiectasia (HHT)

The only guideline-supported exception described in the uploaded review is chronic HHT-related GI bleeding from telangiectases or angiodysplasia that is refractory to usual local measures. In that setting, oral TXA may be considered as part of multidisciplinary HHT management.

This is described as a fundamentally different situation from acute LGIB. The document notes a typical oral regimen of 1,000–1,500 mg three times daily with renal dose adjustment and thrombotic monitoring.

The same review states that non-HHT angiodysplasia use remains investigational and is not standard acute LGIB management.

Suggested Management Focus Instead of TXA

  • Hemodynamic assessment and resuscitation.
  • Blood product support using standard thresholds and procedural targets.
  • Colonoscopy as the primary diagnostic and therapeutic modality when feasible.
  • CT angiography or scintigraphy when colonoscopy is not feasible or nondiagnostic.
  • Angiographic embolization or surgery for refractory bleeding.
  • Correction of coagulopathy and careful antithrombotic management.

Who Might Still Receive TXA?

According to the uploaded review, not patients with routine acute LGIB. The only scenario supported in the document is selected patients with chronic HHT-related GI bleeding, using oral rather than high-dose IV TXA.

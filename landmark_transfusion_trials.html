High-yield practice summary

Quick review

Restrictive RBC transfusion is usually favored

Across ICU care, septic shock, GI bleeding, orthopedic surgery, hematopoietic transplant, and most cardiac surgery populations, restrictive transfusion thresholds are generally safe and reduce blood use.

Common thresholds include about 70 g/L in stable ICU and sepsis patients, 70 g/L in GI bleeding, about 75 g/L in many cardiac surgery pathways, and 70 g/L in hematopoietic transplant populations.

ACS remains nuanced

Acute myocardial infarction is the main adult population where a higher hemoglobin target may be reasonable. Very restrictive thresholds may not be ideal depending on ischemic burden and clinical stability.

Use TXA selectively

TXA shows strong benefit in bleeding trauma and established postpartum hemorrhage. Evidence is mixed in intracerebral hemorrhage and prophylactic obstetrics, and negative for routine GI bleeding or hematologic prophylaxis.

Platelets should not be reflexive

TOPPS supports prophylactic platelet transfusion in hypoproliferative thrombocytopenia, but PATCH and PlaNeT-2 show that platelet transfusion can worsen outcomes in the wrong setting.

Targeted hemostatic therapy is increasing

Cardiac surgery and trauma increasingly compare plasma-based pathways with factor-concentrate strategies, fibrinogen replacement, and algorithm-guided correction.

Trials such as FIBRES, RETIC, and PCC-based cardiac surgery studies support more targeted hemostatic management.

Fresh blood is not better blood

ABLE, RECESS, and TRANSFUSE did not show clinically meaningful benefit from routinely selecting fresher red blood cell units.

How to read this page

Practice-favoring signal: Trial supports the intervention or more restrictive strategy.

Neutral / nuanced signal: No clear superiority, benefit limited to selected outcomes, or practice still individualized.

Practice-changing negative signal: Trial argues against routine use of the intervention.

Cardiac surgery

6 studiesoldest → newest

TRACS

2010
Hajjar et al., JAMA

Population: Cardiac surgery patients.

Comparison: Restrictive vs liberal perioperative transfusion strategy.

  • Restrictive approach was non-inferior for mortality and severe morbidity.
  • Fewer patients were transfused in the restrictive arm.
Take-home: Restrictive transfusion in cardiac surgery is reasonable and helped set up later larger trials.

TRICS-III

2017
Mazer et al., NEJM

Population: Moderate- to high-risk cardiac surgery patients.

Comparison: Restrictive threshold 75 g/L vs liberal threshold 95 g/L in ICU/OR and 85 g/L on ward.

  • Restrictive strategy was non-inferior for death, MI, stroke, or dialysis.
  • Substantially fewer patients were transfused in the restrictive arm.
Take-home: This is a cornerstone trial supporting restrictive transfusion in cardiac surgery.

ATACAS

2017
Myles et al., NEJM

Population: High-risk CABG patients.

Comparison: Prophylactic TXA vs placebo.

  • Reduced transfusion exposure and reoperation for bleeding.
  • No major thrombotic benefit signal, but seizures were increased.
Take-home: TXA is effective in cardiac surgery, but dose and seizure risk matter.

FIBRES

2019
Callum et al., JAMA

Population: Post-CABG bleeding with hypofibrinogenemia.

Comparison: Fibrinogen concentrate vs cryoprecipitate.

  • Fibrinogen concentrate was non-inferior for the number of blood components transfused.
  • No clear difference in death, thrombosis, or treatment-related adverse events.
Take-home: Fibrinogen concentrate is a valid alternative to cryoprecipitate.

PCC vs Frozen Plasma

2022
Smith et al., JAMA Surgery

Population: Cardiac surgery with microvascular bleeding and prolonged PT/INR.

Comparison: PCC 15 IU/kg vs frozen plasma 10–15 mL/kg.

  • Similar bleeding outcomes and overall safety.
  • Better PT/INR correction with PCC.
  • Trend toward fewer transfusions with PCC.
Take-home: PCC is a reasonable plasma-sparing option for selected postoperative cardiac surgery coagulopathy.

FARES-II

2025
Karkouti et al., JAMA

Population: Adults undergoing cardiac surgery with cardiopulmonary bypass and coagulopathic bleeding requiring factor replacement.

Comparison: 4-factor PCC vs fresh frozen plasma.

  • PCC achieved superior hemostatic effectiveness.
  • Reduced bleeding and additional blood product use.
  • No increase in thromboembolic events was seen.
Take-home: PCC may be preferable to plasma for coagulation factor replacement in cardiac surgery bleeding.

Non-cardiac surgery

2 studiesoldest → newest

FOCUS

2011
Carson et al., NEJM

Population: Older patients with cardiovascular risk after hip fracture surgery.

Comparison: Liberal transfusion target >100 g/L vs restrictive target >80 g/L or symptoms.

  • No improvement in death or ability to walk independently at 60 days with liberal transfusion.
  • Liberal strategy increased RBC use.
Take-home: Routine liberal transfusion after hip fracture is not beneficial.

POISE-3

2022
Devereaux et al., NEJM

Population: Non-cardiac surgery patients at risk for bleeding and cardiovascular complications.

Comparison: TXA at the start and end of surgery vs placebo.

  • Reduced major bleeding.
  • Did not meet non-inferiority for cardiovascular outcomes.
Take-home: TXA reduces bleeding in higher-risk non-cardiac surgery, but clinicians still need to weigh CV risk-benefit.

Critically ill

2 studiesoldest → newest

TRICC

1999
Hébert et al., NEJM

Population: Euvolemic critically ill ICU patients with anemia and no active bleeding.

Comparison: Restrictive threshold 70 g/L vs liberal threshold 100 g/L.

  • Restrictive strategy was at least as effective overall.
  • Hospital mortality and cardiac events favored restriction in key analyses.
  • Used substantially fewer RBC units.
Take-home: A 70 g/L threshold is appropriate for many stable ICU patients, with caution in active cardiac ischemia.

TRISS

2014
Holst et al., NEJM

Population: Septic shock with Hb ≤90 g/L.

Comparison: Threshold 70 g/L vs 90 g/L.

  • No significant difference in 90-day mortality or ischemic events.
  • Restrictive arm received far fewer transfusions.
Take-home: Restrictive transfusion is safe in septic shock unless other clinical factors override.

Acute coronary syndrome

2 studiesoldest → newest

REALITY

2021
Ducrocq et al., JAMA

Population: Acute myocardial infarction with anemia.

Comparison: Restrictive strategy around 80 g/L vs liberal strategy around 100 g/L.

  • Restrictive strategy was non-inferior for 30-day major cardiovascular events.
  • Restrictive arm used fewer RBC units.
  • Authors noted the confidence interval still left room for possible harm.
Take-home: ACS may tolerate a moderately restrictive threshold, but the area remained unsettled and called for a larger trial.

MINT

2023
Carson et al., NEJM

Population: Adults with acute myocardial infarction and anemia (Hb <100 g/L) during index hospitalization.

Comparison: Restrictive transfusion strategy vs liberal strategy targeting Hb around 100 g/L.

  • Primary outcome numerically favored the liberal strategy.
  • Cardiac death was lower with liberal transfusion.
  • Restrictive strategy used far fewer RBC units.
Take-home: In MI with anemia, a liberal transfusion strategy targeting Hb around 100 g/L may be preferable, particularly in higher-risk ACS phenotypes.

Obstetrics / postpartum hemorrhage

3 studiesoldest → newest

WOMAN

2017
Shakur et al., Lancet

Population: Established postpartum hemorrhage after vaginal birth or cesarean delivery.

Comparison: TXA 1 g IV vs placebo.

  • Reduced death due to bleeding, especially when given within 3 hours of birth.
  • No clear reduction in hysterectomy.
  • No major safety penalty was shown.
Take-home: Give TXA early in established postpartum hemorrhage.

TRAAP

2018
Sentilhes et al., NEJM

Population: Vaginal delivery.

Comparison: Prophylactic TXA vs placebo postpartum.

  • No significant reduction in the primary PPH endpoint.
  • Some secondary bleeding measures favored TXA.
  • Thrombotic events were not significantly increased.
Take-home: Routine prophylactic TXA after vaginal delivery is not firmly established.

TRAAP2

2021
Sentilhes et al., NEJM

Population: Cesarean delivery.

Comparison: Prophylactic TXA vs placebo after birth.

  • Reduced a measured composite postpartum bleeding outcome.
  • No clear reduction in transfusion or other major clinical endpoints.
  • Nausea and vomiting were more common with TXA.
Take-home: TXA can reduce measured blood loss after cesarean delivery, but major clinical benefit is less clear.

Menstrual bleeding

1 studyoldest → newest

Heavy Menstrual Bleeding Trial

2010
Lukes et al., Obstetrics & Gynecology

Population: Women with heavy menstrual bleeding.

Comparison: Oral TXA vs placebo.

  • Reduced menstrual blood loss.
  • Improved quality of life.
Take-home: TXA remains a useful non-hormonal medical therapy for heavy menstrual bleeding.

Hematologic malignancies and transplant

4 studiesoldest → newest

TOPPS

2013
Stanworth et al., NEJM

Population: Hematologic malignancy or stem cell transplant patients with hypoproliferative thrombocytopenia.

Comparison: No prophylactic platelets vs prophylactic transfusion at platelet count <10 × 109/L.

  • Non-inferiority of no prophylaxis was not shown.
  • Withholding prophylaxis led to more bleeding and shorter time to first bleed.
Take-home: Continue prophylactic platelet transfusion in most hypoproliferative thrombocytopenia settings.

PlaNeT-2 / MATISSE

2019
Curley et al., NEJM

Population: Preterm neonates with severe thrombocytopenia.

Comparison: Higher prophylactic threshold 50 × 109/L vs lower threshold 25 × 109/L.

  • Higher threshold increased death or major bleeding.
  • Major neonatal practice-changing trial.
Take-home: More liberal neonatal platelet transfusion can be harmful.

TRIST

2020
Tay et al., Journal of Clinical Oncology

Population: Hematopoietic cell transplant patients.

Comparison: Restrictive Hb target 70 g/L vs liberal target 90 g/L.

  • Restrictive strategy was non-inferior for health-related quality of life.
  • No major difference in clinical outcomes.
  • Restrictive group used fewer RBC units.
Take-home: Restrictive RBC transfusion is reasonable in hematopoietic cell transplant.

a-TREAT

2020
Gernsheimer et al., Blood

Population: Hematologic malignancy with hypoproliferative thrombocytopenia.

Comparison: Prophylactic TXA vs placebo.

  • No reduction in WHO grade 2+ bleeding.
  • No decrease in platelet transfusion requirements.
Take-home: Routine prophylactic TXA is not supported in this setting.

GI bleeding

2 studiesoldest → newest

Villanueva Trial

2013
Villanueva et al., NEJM

Population: Acute upper GI bleeding.

Comparison: Restrictive target 70 g/L vs liberal target 90 g/L.

  • Improved 6-week survival with the restrictive strategy.
  • Reduced further bleeding.
  • Particularly helpful in Child-Pugh A/B cirrhosis.
Take-home: Stable UGIB should generally be managed with a restrictive threshold.

HALT-IT

2020
Roberts et al., Lancet

Population: Acute GI bleeding.

Comparison: High-dose TXA vs placebo.

  • No reduction in death due to bleeding.
  • More thromboembolic events with TXA.
Take-home: Do not routinely add TXA for GI bleeding.

Intracerebral hemorrhage / neuro

2 studiesoldest → newest

PATCH

2016
Baharoglu et al., Lancet

Population: Spontaneous supratentorial ICH in patients recently on antiplatelet therapy.

Comparison: Platelet transfusion vs standard care.

  • Higher risk of death or dependence with platelet transfusion.
  • Best available trial evidence argues against routine platelet use in this setting.
Take-home: Do not routinely transfuse platelets for antiplatelet-associated spontaneous ICH.

TICH-2

2018
Sprigg et al., Lancet

Population: Intracerebral hemorrhage within 8 hours.

Comparison: TXA vs placebo.

  • No significant improvement in 90-day functional outcome.
  • Generally reassuring safety profile.
  • Possible signal if given very early.
Take-home: TXA has limited proven benefit in ICH, though early use is sometimes considered.

Trauma

5 studiesoldest → newest

CRASH-2

2010
CRASH-2 Trial Collaborators, Lancet

Population: Trauma patients with significant bleeding or at risk of major hemorrhage.

Comparison: TXA vs placebo.

  • Reduced death due to bleeding and all-cause mortality when given early.
  • Benefit was time-sensitive.
Take-home: Give TXA early in bleeding trauma.

Robertson Head Injury Trial

2014
Robertson et al., JAMA

Population: Severe traumatic brain injury / closed head injury unable to follow commands.

Comparison: Hb target 70 g/L vs 100 g/L.

  • No functional recovery benefit with the liberal target.
  • More thromboembolic events with the 100 g/L target.
Take-home: Liberal transfusion to 100 g/L in traumatic brain injury is not supported.

RETIC

2017
Innerhofer et al., Lancet Haematology

Population: Bleeding trauma patients with viscoelastic evidence of coagulopathy.

Comparison: Coagulation factor concentrate strategy vs frozen plasma.

  • Less rescue therapy and less massive transfusion in the factor-concentrate arm.
  • Important proof-of-concept for fibrinogen-first / goal-directed strategies.
Take-home: Goal-directed factor concentrate strategies can outperform plasma-based correction in selected trauma settings.

CRASH-3

2019
CRASH-3 Trial Collaborators, Lancet

Population: Traumatic brain injury treated within 3 hours.

Comparison: TXA vs placebo.

  • Possible mortality benefit in mild-to-moderate TBI.
  • No clear overall benefit in the most severe nonsurvivable injuries.
Take-home: Early TXA is reasonable in selected TBI patients.

STAAMP

2020
Guyette et al., JAMA Surgery

Population: Trauma patients at risk for hemorrhagic shock in the prehospital setting.

Comparison: Prehospital TXA vs placebo.

  • Primary endpoint was neutral overall.
  • Signals of benefit were seen in more severely shocked patients and with earlier treatment.
Take-home: The earlier and sicker the trauma patient, the more compelling TXA becomes.

RBC storage-age trials

3 studiesoldest → newest

ABLE

2015
Lacroix et al., NEJM

Population: Critically ill adults.

Comparison: Fresh RBC units vs standard-issue RBC units.

  • No reduction in 90-day mortality with fresher blood.
Take-home: “Fresh blood” does not improve outcomes in general ICU practice.

RECESS

2015
Steiner et al., NEJM

Population: Complex cardiac surgery patients.

Comparison: Shorter-storage vs longer-storage RBC units.

  • No clinically meaningful difference in outcomes.
Take-home: Cardiac surgery also failed to show meaningful benefit from fresher RBC inventory.

TRANSFUSE

2017
Cooper et al., NEJM

Population: Critically ill adults.

Comparison: Freshest available RBCs vs standard-issue RBCs.

  • No difference in 90-day mortality.
Take-home: Standard inventory issue is appropriate; routine preferential use of freshest units is not evidence-based.

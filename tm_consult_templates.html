IVIG Vital Sign Monitoring

IVIG

Risk-stratified vital-sign monitoring during IVIG infusion.

TRANSFUSION MEDICINE CONSULTATION

Requested by: Dr. [Name]

Reason for consultation:
Review of vital-sign monitoring requirements during IVIG infusion.

Clinical information:
Patient receiving [IVIG product] [dose] every [interval].
Previous IVIG tolerance: [number of prior infusions / reactions].
Relevant risk factors: [cardiac disease / renal impairment / thrombotic risk / IgA deficiency / none identified].

Assessment:
IVIG infusion reactions occur most commonly early in therapy and are frequently rate-related. More intensive monitoring is appropriate for the first infusion, after a product change or prolonged treatment interval, during rate escalation, and in patients with previous reactions or significant clinical risk factors.

For clinically stable patients who have repeatedly tolerated the same IVIG product and infusion rate without reaction, reduced-frequency vital-sign monitoring is reasonable with continued clinical observation and patient education regarding symptoms of infusion reactions.

Suggested monitoring:
Established, low-risk patient:
• Baseline vital signs before infusion.
• Vital signs with clinically significant rate increases according to local infusion protocol.
• End-of-infusion vital signs.
• Additional vital signs immediately if symptoms or clinical concerns develop.

Closer monitoring:
• First IVIG infusion.
• New IVIG product or significant change in infusion protocol.
• Previous infusion reaction.
• Prolonged interval since previous treatment.
• Significant cardiac or renal disease, thrombotic risk, or other clinical instability.

Recommendations:
For this patient, [reduced-frequency / standard enhanced] monitoring is appropriate.
Continue nursing observation throughout the infusion. The patient should promptly report headache, chills, flushing, chest or back pain, dyspnea, dizziness, nausea, or other new symptoms. Slow or stop the infusion and reassess if a suspected reaction occurs.

Communication:
Assessment and recommendations communicated to Dr. [Name] / infusion clinic.

Albumin Use / Fluid Drainage

Albumin

Assessment of albumin replacement after paracentesis and/or thoracentesis.

TRANSFUSION MEDICINE CONSULTATION

Requested by: Dr. [Name]

Reason for consultation:
Assessment of albumin use following [paracentesis / thoracentesis / combined fluid drainage].

Clinical information:
[___] L ascitic fluid removed by paracentesis.
[___] L pleural fluid removed by thoracentesis.
Relevant clinical factors: [cirrhosis / acute-on-chronic liver failure / hypotension / renal dysfunction / electrolyte abnormality / other].

Assessment:
For large-volume paracentesis in cirrhosis (>5 L removed), 20–25% albumin is recommended at approximately 6–8 g per liter of ascitic fluid removed to reduce post-paracentesis circulatory dysfunction.

Albumin dose based on ascitic volume:
[___] L × 6–8 g/L = [___–___] g albumin.
25% albumin contains 25 g/100 mL.

Routine volume-based albumin replacement after thoracentesis, including hepatic hydrothorax, is not supported by established guideline evidence. The paracentesis 6–8 g/L formula should not routinely be applied to pleural fluid volume.

Recommendations:
• For paracentesis >5 L: give 20–25% albumin, approximately 6–8 g/L of ascitic fluid removed.
• Consider albumin with smaller-volume paracentesis when clinically indicated (e.g., hypotension, renal dysfunction, electrolyte abnormalities, or acute-on-chronic liver failure).
• Do not routinely add thoracentesis volume to the albumin replacement calculation.
• Monitor blood pressure, renal function, electrolytes, and clinical volume status.
• Monitor for volume overload, particularly in patients with cardiac or renal dysfunction.

Parvovirus B19-Positive RBC Unit

Infectious Risk

Recipient assessment and follow-up after transfusion of an RBC unit subsequently reported as Parvovirus B19 positive.

TRANSFUSION MEDICINE CONSULTATION

Requested by: Dr. [Name]

Reason for consultation:
Recipient of an RBC unit subsequently reported as Parvovirus B19 (B19V) positive.

Clinical information:
Patient received [unit/component] on [date]. Relevant risk factors: [immunocompromised / hematologic malignancy or transplant / chronic hemolytic disorder / pregnancy / none identified].

Assessment:
Transfusion-transmitted B19V infection is uncommon. Risk depends primarily on the donor viral load and antibody status and the recipient's susceptibility. Clinically significant infection is of greatest concern in immunocompromised patients, patients with increased erythrocyte turnover, and susceptible pregnant patients.

Recommendations:
• Confirm the donor B19V quantitative viral load and, if available, donor anti-B19V IgG status.
• Review recipient risk factors and baseline CBC/hemoglobin and reticulocyte count.
• Monitor CBC/hemoglobin and reticulocyte count clinically over the following weeks.
• If unexplained worsening anemia with reticulocytopenia develops, obtain quantitative B19V PCR; PCR is preferred in immunocompromised patients.
• If infection is confirmed, involve Infectious Diseases/Hematology as appropriate; IVIG may be considered for clinically significant persistent infection in immunocompromised patients.
• Trace and quarantine any untransfused co-components from the implicated donation, as applicable, and follow hemovigilance/reporting requirements.

Assessment and recommendations communicated to Dr. [Name].

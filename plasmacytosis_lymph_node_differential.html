Differential Diagnosis of Plasmacytosis in Lymph Node

Practical pathology reference organized around the core sign-out questions: preserved versus effaced architecture, reactive versus clonal plasma cell proliferation, and the major benign and neoplastic entities that enter the differential diagnosis.

Core Differential

Major diagnostic categories

1. Reactive / polyclonal plasmacytosis

  • Autoimmune and inflammatory disorders, including systemic lupus erythematosus, rheumatoid arthritis-associated lymphadenopathy, Sjögren syndrome, and idiopathic plasmacytic lymphadenopathy.
  • Infectious lymphadenitis, including Epstein-Barr virus, cytomegalovirus, HIV, syphilis, toxoplasmosis, and other infectious granulomatous processes.
  • Castleman disease, especially the plasma cell variant.
  • IgG4-related disease.
  • Rosai-Dorfman disease.
  • Inflammatory pseudotumor and other uncommon reactive conditions.
Typical clues: preserved or only partially distorted nodal architecture, polymorphous cellular background, and polytypic plasma cells.

2. Small B-cell lymphomas with plasmacytic differentiation

  • Nodal marginal zone lymphoma.
  • Lymphoplasmacytic lymphoma / Waldenström macroglobulinemia.
  • Less commonly, chronic lymphocytic leukemia / small lymphocytic lymphoma, follicular lymphoma, or mantle cell lymphoma with plasmacytic differentiation.
Typical clues: monotypic B-cell and plasma cell populations, partial or complete architectural effacement, and correlation with flow cytometry, immunophenotype, and molecular studies such as MYD88 in appropriate cases.

3. Plasma cell neoplasms involving lymph node

  • Extramedullary plasmacytoma.
  • Secondary nodal involvement by plasma cell myeloma.
Typical clues: diffuse infiltration by monomorphic plasma cells, light chain restriction, and supportive systemic clinical findings such as a monoclonal paraprotein, lytic bone lesions, hypercalcemia, renal dysfunction, or marrow myeloma.

4. Large B-cell lymphomas with plasmablastic differentiation

  • Plasmablastic lymphoma.
  • Primary effusion lymphoma / extracavitary primary effusion lymphoma.
  • ALK-positive large B-cell lymphoma.
  • Other aggressive B-cell lymphomas with plasmacytic or plasmablastic features.
Typical clues: large atypical cells, high-grade cytology, frequent loss of mature B-cell markers, and expression of plasma cell-associated markers such as CD138, CD38, and MUM1.

5. T-cell lymphoma with secondary plasma cell proliferation

  • Angioimmunoblastic T-cell lymphoma is the classic pitfall.
Typical clues: plasma cells may be very prominent and can obscure the underlying T-cell lymphoma; look for a TFH phenotype, expanded follicular dendritic cell meshworks, vascular proliferation, and EBV-positive B immunoblasts.
Reactive entities

Important benign mimics

Autoimmune lymphadenopathy

Usually shows preserved architecture with reactive follicular hyperplasia and prominent interfollicular polytypic plasmacytosis. Clinical history is often decisive.

Infectious mononucleosis and other viral lymphadenitides

A polymorphous paracortical expansion with plasma cells, immunoblasts, histiocytes, and sometimes necrosis. Reed-Sternberg-like cells and marked atypia can create concern for lymphoma.

Castleman disease

Plasma cell variant may show striking interfollicular plasmacytosis. Interpretation requires clinicopathologic correlation. HHV-8 and IL-6 context matters.

IgG4-related disease

Consider when there is increased IgG4-positive plasma cells and an elevated IgG4/IgG ratio, keeping in mind that increased IgG4-positive plasma cells are not specific in lymph node.

Rosai-Dorfman disease

Look for sinus/paracortical histiocytes with emperipolesis and an S100-positive histiocytic population accompanying the plasma cells.

Neoplastic entities

Important malignant considerations

Nodal marginal zone lymphoma

Often shows a heterogeneous infiltrate including monocytoid B cells, plasmacytoid cells, and plasma cells, sometimes with Dutcher or Russell bodies.

Lymphoplasmacytic lymphoma

Usually a relatively monotonous lymphoplasmacytic infiltrate, often associated with an IgM paraprotein and marrow disease. MYD88 mutation testing can be very helpful in difficult cases.

Plasmacytoma / myeloma involving lymph node

More monomorphic than reactive plasmacytosis and usually supported by light chain restriction and systemic plasma cell neoplasm findings.

Plasmablastic lymphoma

High-grade morphology, plasma cell phenotype, frequent EBV association, and loss of usual pan-B-cell markers should raise this possibility.

Approach

Practical sign-out algorithm

Step 1: Assess architecture
Preserved architecture favors a reactive process. Diffuse or near-complete effacement raises concern for lymphoma or plasma cell neoplasm.
Step 2: Assess cytology
Polymorphous populations favor reactive conditions. Monomorphic lymphoplasmacytic or plasma cell populations favor neoplasia.
Step 3: Assess clonality
Use kappa/lambda studies, flow cytometry, and correlation with serum findings. Polytypic plasma cells support reactive plasmacytosis.
Step 4: Correlate clinically
Autoimmune symptoms, viral syndrome, elevated inflammatory markers, monoclonal paraprotein, marrow findings, and imaging all matter.
Step 5: Order targeted ancillary studies
Common studies include CD20, CD3, CD138, MUM1, kappa, lambda, EBER, HHV-8, IgG4, flow cytometry, and selected molecular tests.
Pearls

High-yield pitfalls

  • Reactive plasmacytosis can be extensive.
  • Russell bodies and binucleation do not by themselves prove neoplasia.
  • Light chain skewing or even restricted patterns can rarely occur in reactive settings; interpret with caution.
  • Castleman disease and AITL are major mimics.
  • Always integrate morphology, clonality, and clinical context before diagnosing lymphoma or plasma cell neoplasm.
Suggested wording

Descriptive fallback diagnosis

When the findings are not fully classifiable on morphology alone, a practical descriptive line may be:

Lymph node with prominent plasmacytosis / lymphoplasmacytic infiltrate; see comment.

The comment can then address architecture, clonality, key differential diagnoses, and recommend clinicopathologic correlation and ancillary studies.

Reference basis: diagnostic approach to plasmacytic or lymphoplasmacytic infiltrates in lymph nodes, including reactive conditions, small B-cell lymphomas, plasma cell neoplasms, and large B-cell lymphomas with plasmacytic differentiation.

Therapeutic Plasma Exchange (TPE)

A practical prescription-focused guide for hematology and transfusion medicine practice. TPE removes plasma and replaces it with albumin, plasma, or a combined solution. It is most useful when disease is driven by circulating pathogenic plasma constituents such as autoantibodies, alloantibodies, immune complexes, paraproteins, complement factors, cytokines, or selected highly protein-bound toxins.

High-yield points

  • Do not wait for ADAMTS13 before starting TPE if acquired TTP is strongly suspected.
  • Use plasma replacement for TTP; use albumin for most non-TTP indications.
  • Repeated albumin exchange can lower fibrinogen and coagulation factors.
  • Give rituximab, IVIG, monoclonal antibodies, and other critical drugs after TPE when possible.
  • Hyperviscosity from IgM usually responds rapidly.

2. TTP emergency box

Do not delay

Suspected acquired TTP

  • Start TPE urgently if the clinical picture is strongly suspicious.
  • Do not wait for ADAMTS13 activity or inhibitor results before initiating treatment.
  • Use donor plasma as replacement fluid.
  • Exchange 1.0–1.5 TPV daily.
  • Add corticosteroids; consider caplacizumab and rituximab according to local protocol and hematology guidance.
  • Continue daily TPE until platelet count and hemolysis have clearly improved; taper/stop according to institutional TTP protocol.

3. Calculate total plasma volume

TPV

Use a simple Kaplan-style estimate for day-to-day prescribing:

TPV (L) = 0.065 × weight (kg) × (1 − hematocrit)

Example:
Weight = 80 kg
Hct = 0.30

TPV = 0.065 × 80 × 0.70
    = 3.64 L

1.0 TPV exchange = approximately 3.6 L
1.5 TPV exchange = approximately 5.5 L
Practical rule: do not simply increase exchange volume indefinitely. Exchanging beyond approximately 1.5 TPV gives diminishing returns and increases procedure burden.

4. Practical prescription and session design

Order set

Template order

  • Indication: __________
  • Technique: centrifugal / membrane filtration
  • Access: peripheral IV / temporary dialysis catheter
  • TPV: _____ L
  • Exchange volume: 1.0 / 1.5 TPV = _____ L
  • Replacement: albumin 5% / plasma / albumin + plasma
  • Anticoagulation: citrate / heparin / local protocol
  • Frequency: daily / q48h / disease-specific
  • Planned number of sessions: _____, reassess after each session

Pre-treatment checklist

  • CBC, smear if TMA, LDH, bilirubin, haptoglobin as indicated.
  • PT/INR, PTT, fibrinogen, calcium, magnesium, potassium.
  • Group and screen; plasma product planning if plasma replacement required.
  • Baseline disease-specific markers: ADAMTS13, IgM/viscosity, antibody titers, DSA, triglycerides, etc.
  • Review ACE inhibitor use if albumin replacement is planned because of bradykinin-mediated reactions.

Albumin vs plasma
ReplacementUse whenAdvantagesMain cautions

Fibrinogen monitoring

FibrinogenPractical action
>1.5 g/LUsually acceptable to continue planned albumin exchange if no bleeding.
1.0–1.5 g/LMonitor closely; consider plasma component if repeated daily exchange or procedure/bleeding risk.
<1.0 g/LConsider plasma, cryoprecipitate, or fibrinogen concentrate depending on local policy and clinical context.
Any level with active bleedingTreat clinically; consider plasma/cryo/fibrinogen replacement and reassess whether TPE should continue.

6. Clinical indications

High-yield

Hematology

TTP, hyperviscosity syndrome, selected TMA while clarifying diagnosis, selected severe antibody-mediated cytopenias, and selected paraprotein-related complications.

Neurology

Guillain-Barré syndrome, myasthenia gravis crisis, CIDP, severe steroid-refractory demyelinating attacks, and neuromyelitis optica spectrum disorder.

Transplant/immune

ABO-incompatible transplant desensitization, donor-specific antibody reduction, antibody-mediated rejection protocols, and selected autoimmune diseases.

8. Hyperviscosity syndrome

Hematology

Use TPE for symptomatic hyperviscosity: visual disturbance, neurologic symptoms, mucosal bleeding, or severe symptomatic paraproteinemia. Do not delay for a serum viscosity result if the syndrome is clinically obvious.

SettingExpected responseComment
Waldenström macroglobulinemia / IgMExcellentIgM is largely intravascular; one exchange can produce rapid improvement.
IgA myelomaModerate to goodCan cause hyperviscosity; response depends on burden and symptoms.
IgG myelomaLess dramaticIgG has greater extravascular distribution; may require repeated treatment and systemic therapy.
Practical: TPE treats viscosity quickly, but chemotherapy/immunotherapy treats production. Start disease-directed therapy promptly.

9. Drug removal by TPE

Medication timing

TPE most affects drugs that are highly protein-bound, mainly intravascular, or have low volume of distribution. When practical, give critical medications after TPE.

Drug/classLikely TPE removalPractical advice
RituximabSignificantGive after TPE; avoid immediate post-infusion exchange when possible.
IVIGSignificantGive after final exchange or after daily TPE session.
Monoclonal antibodies, e.g. daratumumabLikely significant/possiblePrefer dosing after TPE; coordinate with treating team.
CaplacizumabPossibleFollow local TTP protocol; timing often coordinated around TPE.
SteroidsUsually limited practical impactDo not delay urgent steroid therapy.
BortezomibLikely limitedStill coordinate timing with systemic therapy schedule.
Warfarin / DOACsPossible, especially highly protein-bound agentsMonitor anticoagulant effect clinically/laboratorily when relevant.

12. Plasma donation physician guide

Donation center

This section is for physicians supporting a plasma donation center. It is separate from therapeutic plasma exchange: donor plasmapheresis collects plasma from a healthy donor and returns red cells, whereas TPE treats a patient by removing and replacing plasma. The physician role is mainly donor safety oversight, quality review, staff training, adverse reaction review, and escalation to the medical director when needed.

Core weekly physician presence

  • Provide 4 hours of in-person supervision during normal working hours, preferably when the center is busy.
  • Check in with QA staff and document time on the physician time log.
  • Review outstanding donor visit records in PC-Blut.
  • Review donor adverse reaction records from the previous week.
  • Meet briefly with physician substitutes and donation hall/screening staff.
  • Spend visible time in the donation hall, observe plasmapheresis, and be present for donor reactions when possible.

Daily / as-needed availability

  • Be available for consultation with the medical director with reasonable notice, usually within 24 hours under normal circumstances.
  • Check Grifols email for relevant communications during weekly duties and reply when needed.
  • Notify the medical director of planned or unplanned service gaps.
  • Escalate serious reactions, concerning staff performance issues, policy uncertainty, or donor safety concerns.

Observation and training responsibilities

Physician substitutes

  • Observe each physician substitute at least once per year using the performance observation checklist.
  • Use the encounter as teaching as well as evaluation; deficiencies require additional observation.
  • Focus on donor screening and physical examination sections.
  • Teach a consistent, organized explanation of plasmapheresis, consent, risks, and donor self-exclusion.
  • Ensure the physical examination is performed carefully, not simply documented.

Monthly observations

  • Complete at least one performance evaluation for donor evaluation and physical exam by a physician substitute.
  • Complete at least one observation of donor pre-donation screening by a donor processor.
  • Complete at least one observation of donor phlebotomy/apheresis by a phlebotomist.
  • Discuss concerning observations with the medical director and leave completed forms with QA.

Donor counselling points

Before / during donation

  • Confirm the donor ate and hydrated well.
  • Reinforce water loading, salty snack, and applied muscle tension when appropriate.
  • Explain draw and return phases: anticoagulated whole blood enters the device, plasma is collected, red cells are returned, and saline is given at the end.
  • Tell donors they may stop the process at any time.

Consent and behaviour

  • Ensure the donor understands plasmapheresis purpose, risks, and common side effects such as bruising, pain/numbness, nausea, and citrate symptoms.
  • Emphasize truthful disclosure of health history and risk information.
  • Reinforce zero tolerance for aggressive, abusive, intimidating, intoxicated, or unsafe behaviour.
  • Use the code of conduct discussion to support a safe and respectful environment for donors and staff.

Periodic physician duties

Practical physician mindset: the weekly on-site shift is not only chart signoff. Visible physician presence, constructive staff teaching, and careful review of adverse reactions are the main safety contributions.

13. Pregnancy quick note

Short
Pregnancy: TPE may be used for TTP, severe autoimmune disease, red cell alloimmunization protocols, or severe hypertriglyceridemia when indicated. The major procedural concern is maternal hypotension, which may reduce uteroplacental perfusion. Use multidisciplinary planning and consider gestational-age-appropriate fetal monitoring.

14. Clinical pearls

High-yield

Best targets

Intravascular, high-molecular-weight, slow-turnover targets: IgM paraprotein, pathogenic autoantibodies, complement mediators, and alloantibodies.

TPE is a bridge

It removes circulating material but does not stop production. Pair with steroids, rituximab, chemotherapy, complement blockade, or other disease-specific therapy when indicated.

Albumin vs plasma

Albumin is safer for most indications. Plasma is selected when factor replacement is part of the therapeutic goal, especially TTP.

Daily reassessment

Every session should have a stop/continue decision based on symptoms, platelet count/hemolysis, viscosity, antibody levels, or disease-specific markers.

Fanconi Anemia

 

Q. Fanconi anemia clinical features?

●      Bone marrow failure (80% by age 20)

●      No physical findings (~20%)

○      These patients more likely to be diagnosed in adulthood

●      Short stature

●      Microcephaly

●      Intense patchy brown pigmentation = cafe au lait spots

●      Kidney and thumb malformations

●      Increased → MCV, ADA & HbF

●      Macrocytic anemia

 

Q. What is the pathophysiology of Fanconi anemia?

●      Hypersensitivity to genetic damage → due to a variety of different mutations affecting the fanconi anemia core complex (FANC) or other proteins involved in this DNA repair pathway.

●      Why progressive DNA damage causes aplastic anemia is uncertain and only occurs in some cases → thought to be accumulation of DNA damage in HP

 

Q. Fanconi anemia genetics?

●      At least 15 different genes can be affected.

●      REMEMBER: FANCONI IS From A to N: (FANCA,,,,FANCN)

●      8 FA proteins composing the core complex (A, B, C, D, E, F, G, L, and M) are implicated.

●      >80% of cases involve either FANC-A, C or G genes

●      FANCD1 = severe phenotype

●      FA pathway coordinates a complex mechanism that enlists DNA repair pathways in response to genotoxic insults → eg detects interstrand crosslinks that result in stalled replication fork

 

Q. Fanconi anemia inheritance?

●      Autosomal recessive (usually) or X-linked recessive (FANCB) (rare); FANCR is AD

●      Homozygosity or double heterozygosity need to give rise to disease

●      Very heterogeneous phenotype

Q. Fanconi anemia lab diagnosis?

●      Chromosome breakage studies → phytohemagglutinin-stimulated PB lymphocytes cultured with and without clastogenic agents

●      Report results with chromosomal aberrations compared to control populations.

●      DEB – diepoxybutane

●      MMC – mitomycin C

 

 

Q. What are two clastogenic agents which can be used in Fanconi anemia diagnosis?

●      Mitomycin C and Diepoxybutane

Q. Fanconi anemia and AML association?

●      700 fold increased risk.

●      30% of FA patients have a solid tumor by age 45

Q. What is the main problem with HSCT in Fanconi anemia?

●      1) It is the only treatment for this disease → but only corrects the hematopoietic problems (bone marrow failure and risk of MDS/AML) → the patients are still at risk for non-hematopoietic malignancy as the disease is still present in those cell lines.

●      2) Inherent chromosomal fragility → limits chemotherapy & radiation therapy regimens → conditioning chemo for BMT increases risk of other cancers

●      3) Difficult to find related stem cell donor without familial disease

Dyskeratosis Congenita

 

Q. Diagnostic criteria for dyskeratosis congenita?

●      1) Require 2 of 4 of major features:

○      Abnormal “reticular” skin pigmentation

○      Nail dystrophy

○      Leukoplakia

○      BM failure

AND

●      2) Two or more of the other minor somatic features know to occur in DKC

o   Early hair loss or graying

o   Epiphora

o   Esophageal stricture

o   Hyperhidrosis

o   Hypogonadism

o   Undescended testes

o   Urethral stricture/phimosis

o   Liver disease

o   Malignancy

o   Osteoporosis, aseptic necrosis, scoliosis

o   Pulmonary disease

o   Intellectual disability, developmental delay

o   Microcephaly

o   Cerebellar hypoplasia

o   Ataxia

o   Deafness

o   Short stature

o   Intrauterine growth retardation

 

Q. Dyskeratosis congenita underlying pathophysiology?

●      Telomeropathy

●      9 different genetic abnormalities causing defects in telomere maintenance have been identified.

●      This lack of telomere maintenance causes cellular senescence (aging)

Q. Dyskeratosis congenita inheritance pattern?

●      Can be X-linked (DKC1 → first mutation described), autosomal dominant, or autosomal recessive

●      Genetic heterogeneity

●      Genetic anticipation is prominent → means if you are second generation, your phenotype will be worse and may occur earlier

Q. M:F ratio of DC?

●      10:1

 

Q. Consequences of telomeropathy?

  1. Genomic instability
  2. Stem cell loss
  3. Defective cellular repair and regeneration → liver fibrosis and pulmonary fibrosis

 

Q. How do you diagnose DKC?

●      Flow FISH is used as a screen to identify telomere shortening

○      Single EDTA whole blood sample

○      Screening test

○      If telomeres are below 1st percentile in lymphocytes = abnormal result

●      Sequencing is done to identify specific mutations.

 

Q. What are the 4 main genes involved in dyskeratosis congenita - what is the inheritance pattern for each (2 groups)?

●      X-linked gene

○      The best characterized form of dyskeratosis congenita is a result of one or more mutations in the long arm of the X-chromosome in the gene DKC1 (Think: “K” looks like an “X”)

●      Autosomal dominant genes

○      3 genes: TERC, TERT, TINF2

○      TERT & TERC genes may also be AR

○      Note → in 60% of DC there is no mutation identified

○      TERC, TERT live longer and are more likely to be diagnosed in adulthood

Q. What is Hoyeraal-Hreidarsson syndrome?

●      A severe X-linked form of DKC.

●      Cerebellar hypoplasia

Q. What is the most common clinical manifestation of Dyskeratosis congenita that is NOT part of the triad?

●      Idiopathic Pulmonary Fibrosis

●      Also develop hepatic fibrosis

Q. Main cause of mortality in dyskeratosis congenita?

●      60-70% die of bone marrow failure.

Q. What are the 2 main treatment options for dyskeratosis congenita?

●      Treatment is HSCT or an anabolic steroid called oxymetholone which can improve hematopoietic function in 2/3 of patients

Diamond Blackfan Anemia

 

Q. What is Diamond-Blackfan anemia?

●      Congenital bone marrow failure syndrome which causes → red cell aplasia.

●      Essentially, congenital pure red cell aplasia (ie no other cytopenias)

●      Underlying genetics are poorly understood.

Q. 2 treatment options for DBA?

●      Typically treated with low dose steroids or transfusion support

Q. How do you diagnose DBA - what are the 4 diagnostic criteria? (ie 4 clinical features!)

●      The diagnosis of classic DBA is made if the following diagnostic criteria are met:

1)     Age <1 year

2)     Macrocytic anemia with no other significant cytopenias

3)     Reticulocytopenia

4)     Normal marrow cellularity with a paucity of erythroid precursors

Q. In patients with suspected DBA who do not meet the main 4 diagnostic criteria, what supportive criteria, which are divided into major and minor findings, can be added to make a "probable diagnosis"?

●      Major supporting criteria include:

○      The presence of a gene mutation associated with DBA and a positive family history.

●      Minor supporting criteria include:

○      Elevated ADA activity

○      congenital anomalies associated with DBA,

○      an elevated Hgb F, and

○      no evidence of another inherited bone marrow failure syndrome.

Q. What are the clinical features of Diamond Blackfan anemia?

●      Progressive reticulocytopenia, usually macrocytic anemia in infancy or early childhood.

●      Normal cellularity of the BM with markedly decreased or absent erythroid precursors

●      Congenital malformations (50 percent of patients, see table below)

●      Increased risk of malignancies

●      Increased risk of endocrine dysfunction

●      Range of congenital anomalies observed in DBA

●      Presents typically in first year of life with macrocytic anemia and paucity of red cell precursors on bone marrow examination.

●      Normal cellularity of the BM with markedly decreased or absent erythroid precursors

 

Q. What are the genetics of DBA - name 5 genes[QZ1]  = which is most common, which is X-linked?

Shwachman Diamond Syndrome

 

Q. Underlying pathophysiology of Shwachman-Diamond syndrome - what is the gene?

●      90% of mutation of SBDS gene at 7q11.

●      This gene has an important role in maturation of the 60S ribosomal subunit and therefore SDS is regarded as a disorder of ribosome biogenesis

Q. Shwachman-diamond syndrome bone marrow features?

●      Hypocellular with granulocytic hypoplasia

Q. Shwachman-diamond syndrome clinical features?

●      Pancytopenia often but primarily neutropenia which may be cyclical

●      Pancreatic exocrine insufficiency

●      Growth retardation

●      Skeletal abnormalities

Severe Congenital Neutropenia

 

Q. What is severe congenital neutropenia?

●      A group of disorders that affect granulopoiesis.

●      Typically presents in infancy with severe life-threatening bacterial infections.

Q. How many types of severe congenital neutropenia are there?

●      At least 7 genetic lesions causing this disorder have been identified.

●      The different mutations cause different clinical phenotypes.

Q. What is the most common type of severe congenital neutropenia - what gene is involved?

●      SCN1 is the commonest form of SCN, which accounts for 60-80% of SCN, and the first to be genetically typified.

●      This is an autosomal dominant form that arises from mutations of the ELANE gene on chromosome 19p13.3, which encodes neutrophil elastase.

Q. What is Kostmann disease - which SCN subtype is it?

●      SCN type 3 which has an autosomal recessive inheritance pattern (HAX1 gene)

Q. What is the typical inheritance of severe congenital neutropenia?

●      Variable. The most common type (SCN1) is autosomal dominant.

Q. What is treatment of severe congenital neutropenia?

●      Most cases of SCN respond to treatment with GCSF.

Q. Name 5 congenital causes of thrombocytopenia in children?

●      Congenital TTP = Upshaw-Schulman syndrome

●      Wiskott Aldrich syndrome

●      Gray platelet syndrome

●      TAR syndrome

●      Congenital amegakaryocytic thrombocytopenia (CAMT)

●      MYH9-RD

●      Etc. etc

Q. What is congenital amegakaryocytic thrombocytopenia - inheritance pattern, gene affected and BM findings?

●      AR = a recessive disorder

●      Characterized by severe thrombocytopenia and absence of megakaryocytes in the bone marrow

●      Results from mutations in MPL gene (TPO receptor/CD110)(loss of function, different with gain of function in MPN) and may lead to → trilineage BM failure.

Thrombocytopenia Absent Radii syndrome

 

Q. What is TAR syndrome?

●      Thrombocytopenia with absent radius

●      A genetic disorder which is noted for the absence of the radius bone and thrombocytopenia.

 

Q. What are common physical findings in TAR syndrome?

●      Absence of the radius bone and thrombocytopenia

●      Other findings can include anemia, heart problems, renal conditions.

Q. What is the platelet volume in TAR (large, normal or small PLTs)?

●      Thrombocytopenia with normal platelet volume

Q. What is the mutation in TAR syndrome?

●      Most TAR syndrome cases have a variant and a rare null allele at the RBM8A locus.

●      RBM8A function – splicing and RNA transport, nonsense-mediated decay, cell division.

Pearson syndrome

Q. What is Pearson syndrome?

●      A multiorgan mitochondrial DNA disorder characteristically associated with marrow failure and pancreas dysfunction → neutropenia (mimics Shwachman Diamond syndrome)

●      aka Pearson marrow-pancreas syndrome

●      Treatment is largely supportive, and many patients die in infancy.

●      Mitochondrial DNA (mtDNA) deletion syndromes predominantly comprise three overlapping phenotypes→ Kearns-Sayre syndrome (KSS), Pearson syndrome, and progressive external ophthalmoplegia (PEO).

Constitutional Neutrophil Anomalies

MYH9-Related Disorders

  • Genetics: Autosomal dominant mutations in MYH9.
  • Clinical Features: Macrothrombocytopenia, renal disease, hearing loss, cataracts.
  • Morphology: Giant platelets and faint cytoplasmic inclusions in leukocytes.

Pelger-Huët Anomaly

  • Genetics: Autosomal dominant mutation in LBR.
  • Clinical Features: Typically asymptomatic; may indicate myelodysplasia if acquired.
  • Morphology: Neutrophils with bilobed ("pince-nez") nuclei and condensed chromatin.

MPS I (Hurler Syndrome)

  • Genetics: Autosomal recessive mutations in IDUA.
  • Clinical Features: Coarse facial features, skeletal dysplasia, hepatosplenomegaly.
  • Morphology: Fine azurophilic granules in lymphocytes and granular histiocytes in bone marrow.

MPS II (Hunter Syndrome)

  • Genetics: X-linked recessive mutations in IDS.
  • Clinical Features: Developmental delay and organomegaly without corneal clouding.
  • Morphology: Coarse granules in lymphocytes; plasma cells with inclusions surrounded by halos.

MPS III (Sanfilippo Syndrome)

  • Genetics: Autosomal recessive mutations in genes encoding enzymes for glycosaminoglycan degradation (SGSH, NAGLU, HGSNAT, GNS).
  • Clinical Features: Severe CNS degeneration with mild skeletal abnormalities.
  • Morphology: Clustered coarse granules in lymphocytes; polymorphic inclusions in plasma cells ("Buhot cells").

MPS IV (Morquio Syndrome)

  • Genetics: Autosomal recessive mutations in GALNS or GLB1.
  • Clinical Features: Skeletal dysplasia with normal intelligence.
  • Morphology: Classical Alder-Reilly granulation in neutrophils; fine granules in histiocytes.

MPS VI (Maroteaux-Lamy Syndrome)

  • Genetics: Autosomal recessive mutations in ARSB.
  • Clinical Features: Skeletal deformities, hepatosplenomegaly, corneal clouding.
  • Morphology: Prominent Alder-Reilly granulation in neutrophils and coarse granules in eosinophils.

MPS VII (Sly Syndrome)

  • Genetics: Autosomal recessive mutations in GUSB.
  • Clinical Features: Skeletal dysplasia, hepatosplenomegaly, hydrops fetalis in severe cases.
  • Morphology: Dense granules in neutrophils; coarse inclusions in lymphocytes.

Tay-Sachs Disease

  • Genetics: Autosomal recessive mutations in HEXA.
  • Clinical Features: Neurodegeneration with cherry-red macula, no hepatosplenomegaly.
  • Morphology: Vacuolated lymphocytes without granules; eosinophils with coarse greenish to violet granules.

Mucolipidosis II (I-Cell Disease)

  • Genetics: Autosomal recessive mutations in GNPTAB.
  • Clinical Features: Severe developmental delay, skeletal deformities, and early death.
  • Morphology: "Bag of candy" lymphocytes with rose-pink inclusions in distended cytoplasm.

Mucolipidosis III Alpha/Beta (Pseudo-Hurler Polydystrophy)

  • Genetics: Autosomal recessive mutations in GNPTAB.
  • Clinical Features: Progressive joint stiffness, milder skeletal abnormalities.
  • Morphology: Mixed vacuoles and granules in lymphocytes; coarse, variably colored inclusions in plasma cells.

Neuronal Ceroid Lipofuscinoses (NCL)

  • Genetics: Autosomal recessive mutations in various genes (CLN1–CLN14).
  • Clinical Features: Progressive neurodegeneration with accumulation of lipopigments.
  • Morphology: Vacuolated lymphocytes without granules; occasional coarse granulation in neutrophils.

Alder-Reilly Anomaly (ARA)

Chronic Granulomatous Disease

 

 

What is the pathogenesis is CGD?

-        Inherited disorder with impaired formation of Reactive Oxygen compounds (Superoxide radicals) to kill ingested pathogens

-        Due to defective phagocyte NADPH

-        Phagocytes use NADPH oxidase to generate reactive species of oxygen.

 

What is the mutation causing CGD?

-        CGD arises from mutations that result in the loss or functional inactivation of one of the subunits of the NADPH oxidase complex

-        X-linked CGD

-        CYBB gene (Cytochrome B)

-        Autosomal

-        CYBA

-        NCF1 (Neutrophil Cytoplasmic factor)

-        NCF2

-        NCF4

 

What are the clinical manifestations of CGD?

-        Formation of granulomas

-        Recurrent infection:

-        Pneumonia

-        Abscesses

-        Septic Arthritis

-        Osteomyelitis

-        Bacteremia/fungemia

-        Cellulitis

-        Atypical infections

-        Catalase positive bacteria

-        Staphylococcus aureus, Serratia marcescens, Listeria species, E. coli, Klebsiella species, Pseudomonas cepacia, a.k.a. Burkholderia cepacia, Nocardia.

-        Fungi

-        Aspergillus species, Candida species

-        Other:

-        Growth retardation

-        Chronic pulmonary disease

-        Autoimmune disorders

 

What are the laboratory tests for CGD?

-        Nitroblue Tetrazolium testing

-        Dihydrorhodamine Flow cytometry-based testing

-        Genetic testing

 

Describe the Nitroblue-tetrazolium test

-        Nitroblue-tetrazolium (NBT)

-        It measures NADPH oxidase activity neutrophils after activation with PMA,

-        Reduction of NBT to insoluble blue compound formazan by NADPH oxidase

-        NAPDH is oxidized in same reaction

-        CGD has no formation of blue formazan due to lack of NADPH oxidase

 

Describe the DHR flow test for CGD

-        Dihydrorhodamine testing (DHR) Flow-cytometry based

-        Whole blood stained with DHR & incubated

-        Stimulate neutrophils to produce superoxide radicals

-        Superoxide radicals oxidise DHR to Rhodamine

-        Rhodamine is fluorescent

-        Can be detected by flow cytometer

-        CGD has no/decreased conversion of DHR to Rhodamine due to lack of NADPH oxidase = decreased fluorescent signal compared to normal

Leukocyte Adhesion Deficiency

 

What is Leukocyte adhesion deficiency (LAD)          

●      Rare AR inherited disorder

●      Characterized by → immunodeficiency resulting in recurrent infections

●      Immune defects are supplemented by a Glanzmann thrombasthenia-like bleeding tendency

●      3 subtypes (LAD1, 2 & 3) each due to a different gene defects

●      Neutrophil production increased → marked neutrophilia in blood

●      Adhesion defects →  poor leukocyte chemotaxis & neutrophils unable to migrate to sites of infection

●      Patients suffer from bacterial infections beginning in the neonatal period

○      Omphalitis, pneumonia, gingivitis, and periodontitis

●      Life-threatening due to inability destroy pathogens

●      Cannot form abscesses because granulocytes cannot migrate to the sites of infection.

 

 

 

 

 

●      LAD- I: most common

○      Defect in the common β2-integrin chain (CD18)

○      Leads to decreased adherence to endothelium

○      Characterised by:

■      Recurrent bacterial and fungal infections from birth

■      Gingivitis and periodontitis

■      No pus

■      Delayed umbilical cord separation, or cord stump infection (omphalitis)

■      Neutrophilia

 

●      LAD-2: - rare

○      Defect in GDP-fucose transporter

○      Leads to decreased fucosylation and deficiency of sialyl Lewis X (CD15), which is a ligand for selectins

○      Characterised by:

■      Recurrent infections, but less severe than LAD-I and frequency decreases with age

■      Also has neutrophilia

■      Syndromic features present though: dysmorphic features, short stature, developmental delay

■      Other lab findings: Bombay phenotype!

 

●      Type III - rare

○      Defect in kindlin-3

○      Leads to defective integrin signaling pathway

○      Similar clinical presentation as LAD-I

○      Platelet dysfunction: Glanzmann-like platelet defect

What is the test used for type 1 LAD? What is the principle?

●      Flow cytometry (done at BCCH!)

●      Compare CD11 and CD18 expression on resting granulocytes with granulocytes stimulated with PMA

●      Determine the % expression compared to normal

○      <1% or <2% is severe deficiency

○      2-10% or 2-30% is moderate deficiency

Example reporting:

Interpretation:

The patient's leukocytes show markedly reduced expression of CD18 (5%, reference >90%) and associated β2 integrins (CD11a, CD11b), consistent with a diagnosis of Leukocyte Adhesion Deficiency Type 1 (LAD1). This result indicates defective leukocyte adhesion and migration due to likely mutations in the ITGB2 gene, which encodes CD18.

Suggest confirmatory genetic testing for ITGB2 mutations. Correlate with clinical presentation (e.g., recurrent infections, delayed umbilical cord separation). Consider immunological consultation for management planning.

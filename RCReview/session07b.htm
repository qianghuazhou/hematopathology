Q. What are transfusion indications (threshold criteria) for RBCs for infants under 4 months of age?

●      Acute blood loss of >10% blood volume

●      Hemoglobin <80 g/l → with signs & symptoms of anemia (apnea, bradycardia, tachycardia, decreased vigor, poor weight gain)

●      Hemoglobin <120 g/l → in an infant with respiratory distress syndrome or congenital heart disease.

 

Q. What are indications for transfusion in children above 4 months of age?

●      Essentially the same as adults.

○      Acute blood loss → >15% total blood volume

○      Hemoglobin <70 g/l → with symptoms of anemia

○      Hemoglobin <130 g/l → on extracorporeal membrane oxygenation

○      Significant preoperative anemia → when other corrective therapy is not available

○      Chronic transfusion programs → for disorders of red blood cell production (such as β-thalassemia major and Diamond-Blackfan syndrome unresponsive to therapy)

Q. What is the standard volume of RBC transfusion for infants and small children?

●      10 to 20 ml/kg

●      Typically raise hemoglobin → 10-30 g/L

●      Eg. 10 kg infant → 10 - 20 ml/kg x 10 kg = 100 - 200 mls blood → expect 10-30 g Hb rise

 

 

Q. What is the safety of additive solutions in infants - which additive is of theoretical concern and why?

 

●      Additives are safe in simple transfusions up to 20 ml/kg.

●      Some concerns for massive transfusion that → mannitol is:

○      Nephrotoxic

○      Diuretic

○      Can have possible effects on intracerebral pressure

Q. What is the concern about giving older blood in infants?

●      Older blood has:

○      High levels of → potassium

○      Lower levels of → 2,3-DPG

●      Older blood is safe for typical volumes administered over 3-4 hour

●      Concern exists for larger volume transfusion (>20 ml/kg) or for rapidly administered blood → around potassium load.

Q. How can the potassium in a RBC unit be reduced (3)?

●      In rapid and massive transfusion potassium can be reduced by:

○      1) Supernatant reduction

○      2) Washing the red blood cell aliquots

○      3) Potassium filters exist as well

●      Negating the requirement for fresh red blood cells

Q. What are indications for plasma transfusion in pediatrics?

●      Essentially the same as adults.

●      Replacement therapy → in a bleeding patient or one about to undergo invasive procedure

●      When specific factor concentrates are not available → including but not limited to Factors II, VII, X, and XI, protein C or S

●      PT/INR >1.5 x mid-range of age-related normal value and/or PTT >1.5 x top of age-related normal value → in a bleeding patient or one about to undergo invasive procedure

●      During therapeutic plasma exchange (PLEX) → when Plasma is indicated

●      Reversal of warfarin → in an emergency situation, such as before an invasive procedure with active bleeding  (consider use of prothrombin complex concentrate (PCC) if available PLUS vit K)

Q. What is the composition of the following 3 RBC additive solutions - SAG-M, AS-1 and AS-3 and what is the anticoagulant and approved storage time for these additive solutions?

●      SAG-M = NaCl, Adenine, Dextrose, Mannitol

●      AS-1 = NaCl, Adenine, Dextrose, Mannitol (more dextrose & adenine than SAG-M)

●      AS-3 = NaCl, Adenine, Dextrose (lacks Mannitol). Also contains anticoagulant: Sodium citrate, Citric acid, Sodium phosphate (monobasic)

●      Approved storage time = 42 days for all 3 → eg. question: “What are 3 additive solutions with approved storage time of 42 days” = SAG-M, AS-1 and AS-3

●      Primary anticoagulant-preservative = CPD for all 3

 

Q. What is CPD - what is the storage/shelf life for RBCs in CPD without any additive solution?

 

●      Citrate Phosphate Dextrose (CPD)

●      A solution containing citric acid, sodium citrate, monobasic sodium phosphate, and dextrose

●      CPD is the primary anticoagulant used for preservation of whole blood or red blood cells for up to → 21 days.

●      The official USP name is → “anticoagulant citrate phosphate dextrose solution”

HDFN Hemolytic disease of the fetus/newborn (HDFN)

1. Requirements

a. The “RAT” model

●      1) Response

○      a) Body responds to foreign antigen by making antibody.

●      2) Access

○      a) Antibody formed must be able to cross placenta.

○      b) IgG1, IgG3, IgG4 cross, IgG2 and IgM do not.

●      3) Target

○      a) Target antigen must be present on fetal RBCs.

2. Manifestations

a. Lab findings

●      1) Anemia

●      2) Indirect hyperbilirubinemia/jaundice

●      3) Positive DAT (unless all cells destroyed)

b. “Erythroblastosis fetalis”

●      1) Abundant nucleated RBCs in circulation in response to accelerated destruction

c. Hydrops fetalis

●      1) Most severe form of HDFN, often fatal in utero

●      2) EMH in liver may be so severe as to impair albumin synthesis, leading to hydrops.

 

3. ABO HDFN

a. Most common type of HDFN

b. Generally mild or undiagnosed; Why?

●      1) Weak ABO antigen expression in utero

●      2) Soluble plasma ABO antigens neutralize antibodies

c. Present in first (or any) pregnancy

d. Group O moms, group A or B babies

e. Lab testing

●      1) Cord blood DAT +/-

○      a) Coated cells might be destroyed, or:

○      b) Cells may be very weakly coated

●      2) Antibody screen neg. (screen cells are group O)

●      3) Eluate from fetal cells + with A and/or B cells

f. Treatment

●      1) No intervention is usually necessary, but if severe, treat as outlined under Rh section below.

4. Rh HDFN

a. Prototypical form of HDFN

b. Not first pregnancy (unless mom was transfused)

c. D-neg moms, D+ babies (ABO incompat reduces)

d. Lab testing

●      1) Infant D+ (unless “blocked D”)

○      a) “Blocked D”= false negative D antigen test due to excessive coating of D+ cells with anti-D

●      2) DAT strongly positive

●      3) Antibody screen: anti-D

●      4) Eluate: anti-D

●      5) Indirect bilirubin elevated

●      6) Increased change in amniotic fluid optical density at 450 nm (Liley/Queenan graph)

e. Treatment

●      1) Keep bilirubin < 20

○      a) Lower threshold in premature babies

○      b) Indirect bilirubin can cross the blood/brain barrier and cause kernicterus (basal ganglia bilirubin deposition).

●      2) Phototherapy

●      3) Exchange transfusion with D- blood as necessary

f. Prevention

●      1) RhIG: commercially prepared anti-D

○      a) “RhoGam”, “BayRho-D”: IM forms

○      b) “WinRho”, “Rhophylac”: IV or IM forms

●      2) Prevents D antigen recognition in vulnerable mom

●      3) Obstetric indications

○      a) D-neg female at about 28 weeks gestation

○      b) D-neg female < 72 hours of D+ infant’s birth

○      c) D-neg female with pregnancy complications or invasive procedures (amnio, cordocentesis)

●      4) Contraindications

○      a) D-negative pregnant female who has already made her own anti-D

■      Think about prior RhIG, especially on exams

■      RhIG has a 25-day half-life, but may be detectable for as long as six months

■      Usually distinguishable from maternal anti-D by weak reactions

●      IgM component, and low titer (almost always < 4)

○      b) D+ females

○      c) D-neg mom, D-neg baby (no postpartum dose)

●      5) Dosage

○      a) One full dose vial (300 g or 1500 IU) per 30 ml of D+ whole blood (15 ml D+ RBCs)

■       “Mini-dose” (50 g) sometimes used after 1st trimester abortion for < 5 ml whole blood

○      b) Fetal blood screen (Rosette test)

■      Fetal D+ RBCs coated with anti-D then surrounded by D+ indicator RBCs in rosettes

■      Qualitative (Yes/No)

■      If positive, indicates need for quantitative test

■      If negative, give 1 vial RhIG

○      c) Kleihauer-Betke

■      Quantitative (but poorly reproducible)

■      Acid-resistant Hgb F RBCs stain brightly on blood smear

■      Result is a percentage (% of mom’s blood made of baby blood)

○      d) Flow cytometry

■      Used in some centers rather than KB

■      Samples many more cells and is considered more accurate.

○      e) Using quant. results (Tech Manual Method)

■      i) KB% x blood volume = baby blood in mom

●      Use 5000 ml if no maternal weight given.

●      If weight given, multiply weight x 70 ml/Kg

■      ii) Baby blood vol/30 = vials of RhIg needed

●      Remember, one vial per 30 ml D+ blood

■      iii) Rounding rules

●      If number after decimal is < 5, round up

●      If > 5, round up twice

●      So, “3.4” would mean give 4 vials, while “3.5” would mean to give 5 vials

■      iv) Example: D– mom with D+ baby. KB=2%

●      0.02 x 5000 = 100 ml D+ baby blood

●      100/30 = 3.33

●      Round up once; give 4 vials

○      f) An easier and equally accurate way!

■      i) KB% x 5/3 = number of vials

■      ii) In above example, (2 x 5)/3 = 3.33

■      iii) Note: “5” is mom’s BV in L; use whatever number is appropriate

○      g. Compatibility testing for babies with Rh HDFN

■      1) Mom’s serum may be used to crossmatch.

 

5. HDFN due to anti-K

a. Relatively common; particularly severe

b. Anti-K attaches to K antigen on early RBC precursors and causes severe fetal anemia

c. Less hemolysis than other forms of HDFN (less hyperbilirubinemia and reticulocytosis)

A: Serial titrations should be performed every 6-8 weeks, with considerations for more frequent monitoring in certain cases

Q. Most common antigenic specificity in Fetal alloimmune thrombocytopenia (FNAIT)?

●      HPA 1a (aka PLA1)

●      Usually occurs in a mother who is HPA1b/1b homozygous

●      The HPA-5b - 2nd most common in Caucasians & 2nd most important for NAIT

●      In Asians, the HPA-4a

●      ? Next is HPA-15

 

Pathogenesis of FNAIT

 

●      PLT counterpart of HDFN

●      Must be an incompatibility between the mum and dad of the infant for one of the PLT specific antigens (around 35 described).

●      1st 5 antigens are biallelic system (HPA1-5)

○      “A” allele is high frequency

○      “B” allele is low frequency

○      May vary by ethnic groups

○      They are all SNPs (single nucleotide base differences resulting in single aa change)

●      Classic eg → 85% of cases: mum is HPA 1b/1b homozygous (2%) of population, dad is HPA1a pos (98% of population: hetero a/b of homo a/a)

●      If baby inherits HPA1a from dad and mum is exposed to foreign PLT antigen during pregnancy, she can develop an anti PLT alloantibody

●      The allo ab is IgG → can cross the placenta to bind to fetal PLTs and destroy them (cleared from circ in liver or spleen)

●      Creates risk of thrombocytopenia with severe bleeding → mostly worried about ICH

○      Highest risk is during vaginal birth as baby passes thru birth canal

●      Unlike HDFN → 1st pregnancies can be affected with FNAIT

How do we screen for FNAIT?

●      No screening currently available (unlike HDFN) → issue is discovered in 1st pregnancy following birth of an affected baby with bruising, petechiae, bleeding, thrombocytopenia etc → subsequent work up and Dx

●      Suggested that we could screen all preg women for HPA1a positivity vs negativity

●      Previously no commercially available reagent abs

●      Genotyping a possibility → HPA1a polymorphism has been described and high throughput testing methods are available

●      Now ELISA and flow cytometry with specific antibodies are also available

What are the barriers to screening?

●      Hang up is that there is no preventative treatment like RhIg available if you do identify a pregnant woman at risk

●      Norwegian company is currently developing an anti HPA1a antibody immunoprophylaxis product like RhIg, once this is available, screening might be worthwhile

Incidence of FNAIT

●      Depends on studies

●      1 in 800 - 1,000 pregnancies (primarily from HPA1a incompatibility)

●      Severe thrombocytopenia

●      10 - 20 range

●      May be <10

●      Typically clinical bruising or petechiae present to trigger testing

●      Must exclude ICH if suspicion

Summary

 

●      Severe fetal and/or neonatal thrombocytopenia caused by an antibody from the mother attacking the baby’s platelets in utero. This disease is usually abbreviated as “NAIT,” and has also been called “neonatal alloimmune thrombocytopenic purpura (NATP).” NAIT occurs when a mother lacks a specific platelet antigen (HPA-1a or “PlA1” in 85% or more of cases) and is exposed to that antigen through either pregnancy or transfusion. Mom forms an IgG antibody against the target antigen. In a future pregnancy (or sometimes in the same pregnancy that induced the antibody), the antibody is transported across the placenta and binds to the baby’s antigen-positive platelets. The affected fetus or neonate has severe thrombocytopenia (commonly less than 10,000/mcL), and 20% have intracranial hemorrhage. Several treatments have been described, including intravenous immunoglobulin in mom (prenatal) and baby (antenatal). If the baby must be transfused, there are two main options: 1) Antigen-negative platelets from a random donor, usually obtained from a blood center, or 2) Mom’s platelets (which are antigen-negative), washed to remove the offending antibody, and irradiated to prevent Transfusion-associated Graft-vs-Host Disease.

Q. What is purpura fulminans?

●      It usually is caused by homozygous or compound heterozygous protein C deficiency

●      Characterized by → DIC, venous & arterial thrombosis, & hemorrhagic skin necrosis.

●      Most commonly occurs in → newborns and children.

●      Presents acutely and can be fatal.

 

Q. Mechanism of purpura fulminans?

●      Can be due to congenital or acquired protein C deficiency.

●      Also, in acute severe sepsis or a combination of sepsis and mild protein C deficiency.

●      Protein C has effects on both the coagulation cascade but also systemic inflammatory response.

Q. What is the significance of heterozygous vs homozygous protein C deficiencies?

●      Heterozygotes cause a moderate deficiency and is a moderate risk for thrombosis.

●      Severe deficiencies are associated with fetal CNS and retinal thrombotic events and neonatal purpura fulminans.

Q. How to treat neonatal purpura fulminans?

●      Neonatal purpura fulminans is initially treated with a source of exogenous protein C.

●      Can use FP or protein C concentrate.

●      Infants with purpura fulminans must be treated indefinitely → with an anticoagulation regimen to prevent thrombosis.

Q. What factors are lower in neonates?

●      Anticoagulants Protein C, S, AT

●      Contact factors (XI, XII)

●      Plasminogen

 

Q. What are the von Willebrand levels in neonates - high or low?

●      Neonates have elevated von Willebrand levels which typically normalize around 6 months.

Q. Describe the lymphoid, myeloid, and erythroid compartments in first year of life?

●      There is a relative erythroid hypoplasia in the first year of life → which can be pronounced especially at the physiologic nadir.

Q. When does the physiological RBC nadir occur in neonates?

●      8 to 12 weeks/2-3 months

●      In healthy term infants, the RBC nadir, a physiologic response to postnatal life and not a hematologic disorder, typically occurs at 8 to 12 weeks of life and at a hemoglobin level of 9 to 11 g/dL.

Rhabdomyosarcoma

Q. What are 4 IHC stains for rhabdomyosarcoma?

 

●      MSA+ [Muscle specific actin]

●      Desmin+

●      Myogenin+

●      MyoD1+

Wright-stained bone marrow aspirate smear of a patient with a history of alveolar-type rhabdomyosarcoma demonstrates individually distributed rhabdomyosarcoma cells (black curved arrow). Notice the high N:C ratio with relatively heavy nuclear chromatin and occasional nuclear and cytoplasmic vacuoles.

Wright-stained bone marrow aspirate smear of the same patient demonstrates a cluster of cohesive rhabdomyosarcoma cells (black curved arrow) with overlapping nuclei.

H&E-stained bone marrow trephine biopsy shows extensive marrow involvement by metastatic rhabdomyosarcoma. Notice the residual maturing hematopoietic cells with a perisinusoidal distribution.

Immunohistochemical stain using an antibody directed against desmin shows strong expression of this antigen with membrane and Golgi patterns of staining in rhabdomyosarcoma cells.

Cold agglutinin disease

Q. Definition of cold agglutinin disease

·       Cold agglutinin disease (CAD) is an autoimmune hemolytic anemia mediated by monoclonal cold agglutinins (autoantibodies that agglutinate erythrocytes at an optimum temperature of 0–4 °C) in the absence of any infection or lymphoma.

·       The process is driven by an underlying clonal B-cell proliferation; however, it does not fulfil criteria for a B-cell lymphoma.

Q. What is the pathophysiology of CAD?

·       Cold agglutinin disease (CAD) results from clinically significant IgM cold autoantibodies (cold agglutinins)

Q. What is the titer of usual antibody?

·       Antibody is usually present at high titer (> 1:10,000)

Q. What temperature does the antibody usually react?

·       Antibody is usually reactive with wide thermal amplitude, including at warmer temperatures (> 30 °C)

Q. What phase can cold autoantibody react?

·       Antibody can usually bind complement

Q. What are clinically significant cold autoantibodies?

·       Antibody usually has specificity for I (common), i, or Pr (rare) antigens on red cells

Q. What are 2 categories of CAD?

·       Idiopathic CAD – mostly in older patients

·       Secondary CAD – children and young adults

Q. Secondary CAD can be associated with what?

·       Malignancy – monoclonal gammopathies, chronic leukemias, splenic marginal zone lymphomas, AITL, LPL

·       Infection – EBV (anti-i), CMV, varicella, rubella, HIV; Mycoplasma pneumoniae (autoanti-I); Brucella

Q. How is CAD present?

·       Mild to severe anemia (exacerbated by exposure to cold)

·       Cold sensitivity

·       Cold-dependent acrocyanosis

·       Acral numbness

·       Livido reticularis or organ ischemia

Q. How to diagnose CAD?

·       Positive DAT with C3 only (74%)

·       Small portion with both C3d and IgG antibodies (22%)

Q. What additional testing should be done?

·       Antibody titer and thermal amplitude test

Q. How to treat CAD?

·       Cold avoidance

·       Treat underlying diseases

·       Immunosuppression

·       Red cell transfusion

·       PLEX

Pathophysiology

IgM antibodies (cold agglutinins) bind to red blood cells at low temperatures, leading to agglutination and complement-mediated hemolysis. The destruction of red blood cells occurs in the liver (extravascular hemolysis).

MGRS

Q. What is the definition of MGRS

·       Monoclonal gammopathy of renal significance (MGRS)

·       A Plasma cell or B-cell neoplasm

·       Does not meet the accepted criteria for malignancy or initiation of treatment

·       But secretes a monoclonal immunoglobulin (Ig) or Ig fragment resulting in kidney injury.

·       It is important to note that renal impairment secondary to light chain cast nephropathy in plasma cell neoplasm is a multiple myeloma / plasma cell myeloma–defining event.

Q. What are the diagnostic criteria of MGRS?

·       Essential:

o   kidney biopsy demonstrating injury as a result of a monoclonal Ig;

o   proteinuria > 1 g/day (mostly albuminuria);

o   progressive acute or subacute kidney injury.

·       Desirable:

o   lack of lytic bone lesions;

o   no extramedullary plasmacytoma;

o   no hypercalcaemia secondary to bone lesions;

o   no anaemia with haemoglobin < 10 g/dL;

o   clonal bone marrow plasma cells < 10% of total nucleated cells;

o   involved to uninvolved free light chain ratio < 100; no hyperviscosity;

o   no bulky lymphadenopathy;

o   no thrombocytopenia (< 100 × 109/L).

Q. Clinical presentation of MGRS

·       Kidney always involved

·       Variable proteinuria, hematuria, hypertension, acute or chronic renal insufficiency

Q. Most common disorders associated MGRS?

·       Crystal-storing histiocytosis, crystalglobulin-induced nephropathy, Ig-related amyloidosis, light chain proximal tubulopathy, monoclonal Ig deposition disease, proliferative glomerulonephritis

What are the 6 major subtypes of amyloidosis?

 

  1. Light chain amyloidosis (AL): Also known as primary amyloidosis. Precursor protein is monoclonal immunoglobulin light chains. Associated with plasma cell dyscrasias or mature B-cell neoplasms
  2. Secondary amyloidosis (AA): Precursor protein is serum amyloid A (SAA) protein. Reactive amyloidosis secondary to chronic inflammation, infection, certain neoplasia (renal cell ca) and familial Mediterranean fever
  3. Familial amyloidosis (FAP): Precursor protein is a variant protein such as transthyretin, fibrinogen and apolipoprotein. The most common familial amyloidosis is transthyretin-associated (ATTRm)
  4. Organ-specific: Alzheimer's is the most common. Others: skin, heart, GU
  5. Dialysis related: Protein = beta 2 microglobulin
  6. Age-related (senile) Amyloidosis: Transthyretin depositions in heart. Do better than AL amyloid in heart (75 months vs. 11 months) (ATTRwt)

Comprehensive Panel Recommended by IMWG (WHO5):

  1. High-Risk Abnormalities (Minimal Panel):
    • t(4;14)(p16;q32)/NSD2: Associated with intermediate-to-high risk; outcomes improved with proteasome inhibitors.
    • t(14;16)(q32;q23)/MAF: Strongly linked to poor prognosis.
    • del(17p13)/TP53: Proportion of affected CD138+ cells ≥55-60% correlates with poorer outcomes.
    • 1q21 gain/amplification: Strongly associated with adverse prognosis.
  2. Additional High-Risk and Emerging Markers:
    • t(14;20): Similar to t(14;16) with poor outcomes.
    • Del(1p32)/CDKN2C: Identifies a subgroup with dismal prognosis.
    • TP53 Abnormalities: Biallelic inactivation (mutation + deletion) indicates extremely poor outcomes.
  3. Other Prognostic Abnormalities:
    • t(11;14)(q13;q32)/CCND1: May respond well to BCL2 inhibitors, improving outcomes in this subgroup.
    • Hyperdiploidy: Generally associated with a better prognosis compared to non-hyperdiploid cases.
  4. Advanced Imaging and Biomarkers:
    • Gene expression profiling (GEP): Prognostic but not yet routine in clinical practice.
    • Extramedullary disease and circulating plasma cells: Identified by PET-CT and flow cytometry; adverse prognostic implications.
  5. MRD Assessment:
    • Next-generation flow cytometry or next-generation sequencing for detecting minimal residual disease (MRD).
    • Incorporates imaging like PET-CT to improve the predictive accuracy of MRD status.

What is POEMS syndrome?         

●        It is a paraneoplastic syndrome associated with a plasma cell neoplasm, characterised by:

○        Fibrosis and osteosclerotic changes in bone trabeculae AND

○        Often with LN changes resembling the → plasma cell variant of Castleman disease

●        AKA Osteosclerotic myeloma


What are the diagnostic criteria for POEMS syndrome?       

Mandatory major criteria:

·       Polyneuropathy (typically demyelinating)

·       Monoclonal plasma cell proliferative disorder (almost always lambda)

Major criteria (one required):

·       Castleman disease

·       Sclerotic bone lesions

·       VEGF elevation

Minor criteria:

·       Organomegaly (splenomegaly, hepatomegaly, or lymphadenopathy)

·       Extravascular volume overload (oedema, pleural effusion, or ascites)

·       Endocrinopathy (adrenal, thyroid, pituitary, gonadal, parathyroid, pancreatic)

·       Skin changes

·       Papilloedema

·       Thrombocytosis/polycythaemia

For the diagnosis of POEMS syndrome, one major criterion and one or more minor criteria must be met, in addition to both mandatory criteria.

 
What is the etiology of POEMS syndrome?         

●        Not well understood!

●        Elevated VEGF levels play a role.

What are the characteristic bone marrow changes seen in POEMS syndrome?         

●        A single or multiple osteosclerotic plasmacytoma

○        Composed of focally thickened trabecular bone with associated para trabecular fibrosis containing entrapped plasma cells.

●        Plasma cells are elongated due to distortion by small bands of connective tissue

●        Away from the bony trabeculae, plasma cells are usually <5% but can be more than 50% in disseminated disease

●        Lymphoid aggregates → rimmed by monotypic or polytypic plasma cells in half of patients.

○        Target like appearance

●        Megakaryocyte hyperplasia in clusters and often with atypical morphology similar to those seen in MPNs.

●       

●        B: CD138, C:lambda, D: Kappa

What are the microscopic features of LN biopsy in POEMS?         

●        Features similar to the plasma cell variant of Castleman disease (HHV8)

What is the immunophenotype in POEMS         

●        Monoclonal plasma cell population: IgG or IgA type, lambda restricted (TEMPI is kappa)

What is the prognosis of POEMS syndrome?         

●        Chronic and progressive disease.

●        Median OS is as long as 165 months and a 5-year survival rate of 60-94%

Blood Products

Reference page for major transfusion products including red blood cells, platelets, plasma, cryoprecipitate, and granulocytes, with product-specific indications, compatibility considerations, practical dosing notes, and common cautions.

This page is intended as a practical transfusion medicine reference. Local institutional policies, product availability, and patient-specific factors should always be considered.

General Principles

Use the specific product needed

Transfuse the component that corrects the clinical problem rather than using blood products empirically. For example, use RBCs for oxygen-carrying support, platelets for thrombocytopenia or platelet dysfunction, plasma for multiple coagulation factor deficiencies, and cryoprecipitate for hypofibrinogenemia.

Transfusion is not benign

Risks include volume overload, hemolytic reactions, febrile and allergic reactions, TRALI, TACO, alloimmunization, infection risk, and metabolic complications.

Choose modified products when indicated

Depending on the clinical scenario, products may need leukoreduction, irradiation, washing, CMV-risk reduction, antigen matching, or HLA matching.

Common product modifications
  • Leukoreduced: reduces febrile non-hemolytic transfusion reactions, HLA alloimmunization, and CMV transmission risk.
  • Irradiated: prevents transfusion-associated graft-versus-host disease by inactivating donor lymphocyte proliferation.
  • Washed: reduces plasma proteins; useful in selected cases such as recurrent severe allergic/anaphylactic reactions or IgA deficiency with anti-IgA.
  • Antigen-matched: often used in chronically transfused patients, especially sickle cell disease, to reduce alloimmunization.

Red Blood Cells (RBCs) Cellular Component

Packed red blood cells are used to increase oxygen-carrying capacity in anemia or blood loss. They are the most commonly transfused component.

Contents
  • Concentrated red cells suspended in additive solution
  • Minimal plasma compared with whole blood
  • Usually leukoreduced in Canada and many other jurisdictions
Main indications
  • Symptomatic anemia
  • Acute hemorrhage
  • Perioperative blood loss or anemia affecting tissue oxygen delivery
  • Chronic transfusion support, such as in sickle cell disease or marrow failure states
  • Exchange transfusion settings in selected disorders
Typical practice points
  • In adults, 1 unit usually increases hemoglobin by about 10 g/L.
  • In infants and small children, 10–15 mL/kg typically increases hemoglobin by about 20–30 g/L.
  • Restrictive transfusion strategies are generally preferred unless there is active bleeding, ischemia, or another specific indication.
  • RBC transfusion should not be used simply as a volume expander when oxygen-carrying support is not needed.
Important cautions
  • Requires ABO compatibility and appropriate pretransfusion testing.
  • Risk of hemolytic transfusion reaction if incorrect unit is administered.
  • May contribute to TACO, hyperkalemia, hypothermia, iron overload, and alloimmunization.
  • In chronic transfusion settings, monitor ferritin and iron burden.
Product-specific situations
  • Irradiated RBCs: for stem cell transplant recipients, selected immunocompromised patients, intrauterine transfusion, and other recognized indications.
  • Washed RBCs: for selected patients with severe allergic or anaphylactic reactions, especially when related to plasma proteins.
  • Phenotype- or genotype-matched RBCs: especially important in sickle cell disease and selected alloimmunized patients.
  • CMV-risk-reduced RBCs: commonly met by leukoreduced components in many settings.

Platelets Cellular Component

Platelet transfusion is used for prevention or treatment of bleeding due to thrombocytopenia or clinically important platelet dysfunction.

Platelet basics
  • Manufacturing: Platelets are derived from whole blood donations (pooled/buffy coat products) or collected by apheresis from a single donor.
  • Pathogen-reduced platelets: May be treated with psoralen/UV technology and are commonly stored in platelet additive solution (PAS), depending on local inventory.
  • Storage: Stored at room temperature with continuous agitation.
  • Shelf life: Commonly up to 7 days, although pathogen-reduced products may have shorter local expiry depending on manufacturing practice.
  • Administration: One adult dose is usually 1 apheresis unit or 1 pooled platelet dose, typically infused over about 30–60 minutes and completed within 4 hours.
Types of platelet products
  • Apheresis platelets: single donor collection
  • Pooled platelets: pooled from multiple whole blood donations
  • Pathogen-reduced platelets: available in some centres
  • Products may also be HLA-matched, crossmatch-compatible, washed, irradiated, or PAS-based depending on availability and indication
Main indications
  • Prophylaxis in severe thrombocytopenia
  • Treatment of active bleeding associated with thrombocytopenia
  • Support before invasive procedures or surgery
  • Selected cases of qualitative platelet dysfunction with bleeding
  • Massive hemorrhage protocols
Common thresholds
  • ≤10 × 109/L: prophylaxis for stable non-bleeding patients with hypoproliferative thrombocytopenia
  • ≤20 × 109/L: often used in higher-risk settings such as fever, sepsis, mucosal bleeding, or added bleeding risk
  • <20 × 109/L: central venous catheter placement in many practices
  • <20–50 × 109/L: procedures with lower expected blood loss, depending on site and operator
  • <50 × 109/L: active bleeding, major non-neuraxial surgery, or significant invasive procedures
  • >80 × 109/L: epidural or neuraxial anesthesia in many practices
  • <100 × 109/L: neurosurgery, central nervous system bleeding, intracranial hemorrhage, or traumatic brain injury
Selected therapeutic situations
  • Antiplatelet drug-associated bleeding: platelet transfusion may be considered in selected severe bleeding scenarios; practice depends on the agent, timing of last dose, and clinical context.
  • Massive hemorrhage: platelet support is often guided by protocol, clinical bleeding, and serial laboratory results.
  • Qualitative platelet dysfunction: may be considered when there is clinically important bleeding or urgent intervention.
Situations where platelet transfusion may be ineffective or avoided
  • Immune thrombocytopenia (ITP), unless there is serious bleeding or an urgent procedure
  • Thrombotic thrombocytopenic purpura (TTP), unless life-threatening bleeding
  • Heparin-induced thrombocytopenia (HIT), generally avoided unless major bleeding
  • Post-transfusion purpura
  • Uremic bleeding is usually better addressed with other measures unless thrombocytopenia is also present
Risks of platelet transfusion
  • Common: febrile non-hemolytic transfusion reactions and minor allergic reactions
  • Serious but uncommon: bacterial contamination/sepsis, TRALI, hemolytic reactions, and other severe transfusion reactions
  • Alloimmunization: HLA alloimmunization can lead to platelet refractoriness, especially with repeated transfusions
Compatibility and product selection
  • ABO compatibility: ABO-identical platelets are preferred when possible because they generally provide better post-transfusion increments.
  • Minor ABO incompatibility: plasma reduction or PAS platelets may help reduce hemolytic risk in selected settings.
  • Rh compatibility: RhD-negative females of childbearing potential who receive RhD-positive platelets may require Rh immune globulin prophylaxis, depending on product type and local policy.
  • Leukoreduction: standard in many jurisdictions and helps reduce alloimmunization, febrile reactions, and CMV transmission risk.
Special platelet products
  • HLA-matched or crossmatch-compatible platelets: for platelet refractoriness due to alloimmunization
  • HPA-selected platelets: considered in selected patients with anti-HPA antibodies
  • Washed platelets: for recurrent severe allergic reactions, IgA deficiency with anti-IgA when IgA-deficient donor platelets are unavailable, or selected neonatal settings
  • Irradiated platelets: for patients at risk for transfusion-associated graft-versus-host disease (TA-GVHD)
  • Maternal washed platelets: may be used in neonatal alloimmune thrombocytopenia in selected situations
Platelet refractoriness
  • Definition: repeated poor platelet count increments after transfusion
  • Non-immune causes are more common: sepsis, fever, bleeding, DIC, splenomegaly, consumption, medications, and critical illness
  • Immune causes: most often anti-HLA antibodies; less commonly anti-HPA antibodies
  • Timing of increment: a poor 1-hour corrected count increment suggests an immune cause, whereas a poor later increment is more often due to non-immune consumption or sequestration
  • Management: confirm refractoriness, assess for clinical causes, and use HLA-matched, HPA-selected, or crossmatch-compatible platelets when appropriate
Alternatives and adjuncts
  • Antifibrinolytics such as tranexamic acid may be useful in selected bleeding settings
  • DDAVP may help in some qualitative platelet dysfunction states
  • Topical hemostatic measures can be useful for localized bleeding
  • These do not replace platelet transfusion when there is severe thrombocytopenia with active bleeding or urgent invasive need
Practical note
  • A poor post-transfusion increment may reflect bleeding, sepsis, splenomegaly, DIC, fever, consumption, drug effect, or immune refractoriness.
  • Restrictive prophylactic transfusion thresholds are generally preferred, and unnecessary platelet transfusions should be avoided in TTP, HIT, and uncomplicated ITP.

Plasma (FFP / FP24 / Thawed Plasma) Plasma Component

Plasma provides multiple coagulation factors and is used when there is clinically relevant coagulation factor deficiency, usually affecting several factors at once.

Contents
  • All soluble coagulation factors
  • Albumin and other plasma proteins
  • Minimal or no viable cellular content
Main indications
  • Active bleeding with multiple coagulation factor deficiency
  • Massive transfusion
  • DIC with bleeding
  • Urgent warfarin reversal if PCC is unavailable or insufficient
  • Therapeutic plasma exchange in TTP
  • Liver disease with bleeding or prior to urgent invasive procedure in selected cases
What plasma is not for
  • Routine volume expansion
  • Nutritional protein replacement
  • Mild INR prolongation without bleeding or procedure
  • Correction of laboratory abnormalities alone without a clinical indication
Compatibility and dosing
  • AB plasma is the universal plasma donor.
  • Plasma should generally be ABO compatible with the recipient.
  • Typical dose is 10–15 mL/kg.
  • Small doses are often inadequate to meaningfully change coagulation factor levels.
Important cautions
  • May cause allergic reactions, TRALI, and TACO.
  • Large volumes may be required to correct coagulopathy, especially in adults.
  • Not a substitute for targeted products such as fibrinogen replacement when the major deficiency is low fibrinogen.

Cryoprecipitate Fibrinogen-Rich Component

Cryoprecipitate is prepared from plasma and contains concentrated fibrinogen and selected other proteins. Its main current role is fibrinogen replacement.

Contents
  • Fibrinogen
  • Factor VIII
  • von Willebrand factor
  • Factor XIII
  • Fibronectin
Main indications
  • Hypofibrinogenemia with bleeding
  • Massive hemorrhage with low fibrinogen
  • DIC with fibrinogen depletion
  • Inherited fibrinogen deficiency when fibrinogen concentrate is unavailable
Practical thresholds
  • Often considered when fibrinogen is <1.5–2.0 g/L in bleeding patients, depending on context.
  • In obstetric hemorrhage and major bleeding, higher fibrinogen targets may be used.
Important points
  • Cryoprecipitate is generally preferred over plasma when the principal issue is fibrinogen deficiency.
  • Volume is much lower than plasma for fibrinogen replacement.
  • ABO compatibility is often preferred when feasible, but is generally less critical than for plasma or RBCs.

Granulocytes Specialized Cellular Component

Granulocyte transfusions are uncommon and are reserved for selected patients with severe neutropenia and serious infection when standard therapy is insufficient and marrow recovery is expected.

Main indications
  • Profound neutropenia, often <0.5 × 109/L
  • Documented severe bacterial or fungal infection
  • Failure to respond adequately to antimicrobial therapy
  • Reasonable expectation of marrow recovery
Important characteristics
  • Short shelf life
  • Usually given soon after collection
  • Should be irradiated
  • ABO compatibility is preferred
  • Crossmatch may be required because of red cell contamination
Important cautions
  • May cause febrile reactions, alloimmunization, pulmonary reactions, and volume-related complications.
  • Use is highly specialized and often guided directly by transfusion medicine and the treating service.

Albumin Plasma-Derived Protein Product

Albumin is a human-derived plasma protein product used in selected indication-specific settings. It should not be treated as a routine first-line volume expander. Current guidance emphasizes restrictive, evidence-based use, with most common historical uses not supported for routine practice.

What albumin is
  • Manufactured from donated human plasma
  • Used for oncotic support or selected disease-specific indications
  • More costly than crystalloids
  • Potential adverse effects include fluid overload, hypotension, hemodilution, and anaphylaxis
High-yield practice message
  • Albumin is generally a second-line, indication-specific product rather than a general-purpose resuscitation fluid.
  • Routine use in critical care, perioperative care, dialysis, or cirrhosis outside defined indications is usually not supported.
  • Low serum albumin alone is not an indication for albumin infusion.
Where guideline support is strongest
  • Large-volume paracentesis (> 5 L): suggested to reduce paracentesis-induced circulatory dysfunction
  • Spontaneous bacterial peritonitis: suggested as adjunctive therapy in selected patients with cirrhosis
  • Therapeutic plasma exchange: replacement fluid in selected exchange protocols
Common clinical uses not routinely supported
  • Routine intravascular volume expansion when crystalloids are adequate
  • Non-specific hypotension without a defined indication
  • Correction of hypoalbuminemia alone
  • Routine use in sepsis, dialysis, or cardiovascular surgery to improve outcomes
  • Repeated albumin to normalize serum albumin in decompensated cirrhosis
5% vs 25% albumin
  • 5% albumin: iso-oncotic; generally thought of as an intravascular volume expander
  • 25% albumin: hyperoncotic; generally used when oncotic mobilization is desired, especially in cirrhosis-related applications
  • Important limitation: current evidence does not clearly define the optimal formulation, dose, target albumin level, or schedule for most indications
  • Selection of 5% vs 25% is therefore usually based on indication-specific practice convention rather than strong comparative guideline evidence
Practical decision approach
  • Step 1: Ask whether albumin is needed at all. In many settings, crystalloids or other standard therapy are preferred.
  • Step 2: If albumin is indicated, define the goal: intravascular expansion vs oncotic support / fluid mobilization.
  • Step 3: For volume expansion, 5% albumin may be used in selected settings, although routine first-line use is generally not supported.
  • Step 4: For cirrhosis-related indications such as large-volume paracentesis or spontaneous bacterial peritonitis, 25% albumin is commonly used in practice.
  • Step 5: Reassess volume status, hemodynamics, renal function, and risk of fluid overload after each course.
Clinical framing by setting
  • Cirrhosis: this remains the most important indication-specific area for albumin use.
  • Critical illness / sepsis: routine front-line use is not supported; rescue use after substantial crystalloid exposure remains an area of uncertainty.
  • Kidney replacement therapy: routine use to prevent intradialytic hypotension or improve ultrafiltration is not well supported.
  • Cardiovascular surgery: evidence does not show clear outcome benefit compared with less costly alternatives.
  • Hepatorenal syndrome / HRS-AKI: albumin is commonly used clinically with vasoconstrictor-based treatment strategies, but direct randomized comparative evidence remains limited.
Practical cautions
  • Albumin is not benign; monitor for volume overload and infusion-related reactions.
  • Use indication-based dosing rather than reflex ordering.
  • When simple crystalloids are adequate, albumin is usually unnecessary.
Bottom line
  • Use albumin selectively for clearly defined indications, especially in cirrhosis-related complications and selected exchange procedures.
  • The key question is usually whether albumin should be used at all, not simply which concentration to choose.

FARES-II Trial

Prothrombin Complex Concentrate vs Frozen Plasma for Coagulopathic Bleeding in Cardiac Surgery

Karkouti et al. JAMA. 2025. doi:10.1001/jama.2025.3501

Clinical Question

In adults undergoing cardiac surgery with cardiopulmonary bypass, is use of a 4-factor prothrombin complex concentrate (PCC), compared with fresh frozen plasma (FFP), safe and effective for treatment of active or anticipated coagulopathic bleeding?

Background

  • Bleeding after cardiac surgery is common and frequently requires transfusion of blood components and factor replacement.
  • Postoperative bleeding is associated with increased morbidity and mortality.
  • Coagulation factor depletion is an important contributor to post-cardiopulmonary bypass bleeding.
  • FFP has historically been the standard product for factor replacement, but robust evidence for haemostatic effectiveness is limited.
  • FFP also carries recognized adverse effects, including serious and potentially life-threatening complications.
  • 4-factor PCC contains factors II, VII, IX, and X, with small amounts of proteins C and S, and offers several practical advantages:
    • standardized coagulation factor content
    • purification and concentration
    • pathogen reduction
    • no need for thawing
    • smaller infusion volume
  • Recent data suggested PCC may be an alternative to plasma, prompting this phase 3 trial.

Study Design

  • Phase 3, multicenter, parallel-group, randomized controlled trial
  • Unblinded treating clinicians
  • Patients and outcome assessors were blinded to treatment allocation
  • 1:1 randomization to PCC or frozen plasma
  • Permuted block randomization stratified by site
  • Randomization occurred when an order for PCC or FFP was received by the blood bank
  • Treatment allocation was maintained in the blood bank or pharmacy in sequential opaque sealed envelopes
  • To preserve allocation concealment, investigational products were stored in tamper-proof boxes and transported to the operating room unopened until the final decision to administer product had been made
  • Funded by Octapharma AG and the Canadian Institutes of Health Research

Sample Size

  • 500 participants were required for 90% power to demonstrate noninferiority for the primary outcome of haemostatic effectiveness
  • Assumed haemostatic effectiveness:
    • PCC: 70%
    • Frozen plasma: 65%
  • Noninferiority margin: 0.10
  • One-sided α: 0.025
  • Predicted dropout rate: 20%

Setting

  • 12 sites in Canada and the United States
  • November 30, 2022 to May 28, 2024

Population

Inclusion Criteria

  • Adults undergoing cardiac surgery
  • Use of cardiopulmonary bypass
  • Clinician requested either FFP or PCC after termination of cardiopulmonary bypass

Exclusion Criteria

  • Heart transplant
  • Insertion or removal of ventricular assist devices
  • Repair of thoraco-abdominal aneurysm
  • Any concomitant non-cardiac surgery

Enrollment

  • 538 patients screened
  • 528 randomized
  • Primary analysis:
    • 213 in the PCC group
    • 207 in the FFP group
  • 108 excluded after randomization:
    • did not receive either treatment: n=60
    • did not meet inclusion criteria: n=56

Baseline Characteristics

Baseline characteristics were well matched between the PCC and FFP groups.

  • Median age: 67 vs 64 years
  • Female: 26.3% vs 26.6%
  • Hemoglobin: 13.7 vs 13.6 g/dL
  • Platelets: 201 vs 199
  • INR: 1.1 vs 1.1
  • Similar case mix in elective vs emergency procedures
  • Similar surgical profiles, including graft and valve procedures
  • Mean cardiopulmonary bypass duration: 171 vs 176 minutes

Intervention

4-factor prothrombin complex concentrate (PCC)

  • Most formulations contained factors II, VII, IX, X, and small amounts of proteins C and S and heparin
  • Intravenous PCC dose: 24 IU/kg
  • Maximum of 2 doses within 24 hours of initiation
  • Fixed initial dose:
    • 1500 IU if weight ≤60 kg
    • 2000 IU if weight >60 kg
  • Maximum cumulative dose approximately 50 IU/kg

Control

Fresh frozen plasma (FFP)

  • 3 units if weight ≤60 kg
  • 4 units if weight >60 kg
  • Maximum of 2 doses within 24 hours of initiation
  • Non-investigational PCC was permitted in the control group, up to a maximum of 50 IU/kg, if the treating team felt it was necessary after the initial 2 doses of investigational FFP

Management Common to Both Groups

  • The final decision to administer investigational product was based on a combination of:
    • at least moderate bleeding using a validated score
    • INR result
  • For any further factor replacement, both groups could receive non-investigational FFP as directed by the treating team
  • Established local bleeding and transfusion protocols at each site were not modified
  • Antifibrinolytic agents were routinely used at all sites

Primary Outcome

Haemostatic response, defined as no additional haemostatic intervention required from 60 minutes to 24 hours after initiation of the first dose of PCC or FFP.

Haemostatic interventions included:

  • surgical re-opening for bleeding
  • administration of a second dose of PCC or FFP
  • transfusion of any allogeneic blood products other than red blood cells
  • transfusion of coagulation factor concentrates
Haemostatic effectiveness was achieved in 77.9% of the PCC group versus 60.4% of the FFP group.

This corresponded to a significant reduction in haemostatic failure with PCC: RR 0.56; 95% CI, 0.41-0.75; P<0.001.

Components of Haemostatic Response

  • Surgical re-opening for bleeding: 5.2% vs 7.3%
  • Second dose of PCC/FFP: 8.9% vs 19.3%
  • PCC given outside trial protocol: 0% vs 6.8%
  • Platelet transfusion: 15.0% vs 30.4%
  • Factor VII use: 0% vs 4.4%

Ineffective haemostatic response in all prespecified subgroup analyses and in the per-protocol analysis also favored PCC.

Secondary Outcomes

Compared with FFP, the PCC group had significantly lower:

  • Severe to massive bleeding (class III or IV haemorrhage): RR 0.51 (95% CI, 0.31-0.76), P = 0.001
  • Cumulative requirement for allogeneic blood products and non-investigational PCC/FFP
  • Chest tube drainage at 12 hours postoperatively (mean difference 171 mL less)
  • Chest tube drainage at 24 hours postoperatively (mean difference 232 mL less)

Safety Outcomes

  • Serious adverse events attributed to treatment were significantly lower in the PCC group: 36.2% vs 47.3%, RR 0.76 (95% CI, 0.61-0.96), P = 0.02
  • No significant difference in:
    • death
    • thromboembolic events
    • duration of mechanical ventilation
    • ICU length of stay
    • hospital length of stay

Authors’ Conclusions

PCC had superior haemostatic efficacy and may have safety advantages compared with frozen plasma in patients requiring coagulation factor replacement for bleeding during cardiac surgery.

Strengths

  • Well-designed, multicenter randomized trial in an important high-risk bleeding population
  • The PCC dose used reflected routine clinical practice and may explain the observed difference between groups
  • Good allocation concealment
  • Outcome assessors were blinded
  • Pragmatic design allowed rapid randomization and administration in the operating room
  • Local bleeding and transfusion protocols were preserved, improving real-world relevance
  • Routine antifibrinolytic use reflected standard practice
  • Appropriate safety assessment included serious adverse events and thromboembolic complications

Weaknesses

  • Clinicians were unblinded, although efforts were made to conceal allocation until the point of administration
  • Non-standardized transfusion practice may have introduced within-group heterogeneity in timing, sequence, and rate of product administration
  • About 20% of randomized patients were excluded after randomization, although many were excluded because the intervention was ultimately not needed
  • The primary outcome, haemostatic efficacy, is not a universally established or validated endpoint and is a composite surrogate for more clinically important outcomes
  • Findings may not generalize outside the cardiac surgical population
  • Findings may not apply to patients with recent thromboembolic events, in whom PCC may remain a concern because of prothrombotic potential

Bottom Line

In coagulopathic bleeding after cardiac surgery, PCC was superior to FFP for haemostatic effectiveness and was associated with reduced bleeding, lower use of additional blood products, and fewer serious adverse events, without an apparent increase in thromboembolic complications.
  • FARES-II provides strong evidence supporting preferential use of PCC over plasma in this setting.
  • The smaller infusion volume and faster administration of PCC are practical advantages, especially when volume overload or right ventricular dysfunction is a concern.
  • Further cost-effectiveness work is needed, since PCC is substantially more expensive than plasma, and this may affect broader adoption and generalizability.
